*This started as [a comment](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/links-for-february-2026/comment/210341389) on Scott Alexander's [Links for February 2026](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/links-for-february-2026), expanding on item #60 about cancer chemicals that dissolve Alzheimer's plaques. Scott [recommended it](https://www.astralcodexten.com/p/open-thread-420) in Open Thread 420.*

Cancer patients get Alzheimer's less often than you'd expect. This has been floating around epidemiology for about fifteen years. A [nationwide Danish cohort](https://doi.org/10.1002/alz.12090) of over two million people found a modest inverse association — survivors of various cancers had somewhat lower AD risk. A US veterans study saw the same pattern. Multiple groups tried adjusting for age, follow-up time, and competing mortality. The effect shrank but never quite disappeared. The default explanations were satisfyingly boring: cancer patients die younger, so they never live long enough to develop dementia. Shared genetic risk factors pull in opposite directions. Doctors monitoring cancer patients catch cognitive decline at different rates. The correlation was real enough to generate papers but never seemed to require a biological mechanism. Statistical artifact, survivorship effect, move on.

Then a [2026 paper in Cell](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2025.12.020) found a biological mechanism. Mouse tumors secreted cystatin C into the bloodstream. The cystatin C crossed into the brain, activated TREM2 on microglia, which migrated to amyloid plaques and degraded them. Delete TREM2, and the effect vanished. The tumors were producing brain medicine.

Why would a cancer do this?

You'd naturally treat cancer and neurodegeneration as opposite diseases. From the cell's perspective, they're the same problem: a body full of aging cells accumulating mutations.

Each cell in a multicellular body can bring down the entire organism by hotwiring its "multiply right now" button to always be on. Multicellular life evolved permission-slip style systems — tumor suppressors — that strictly control when replication is allowed. Young bodies have the same genome in every cell, the full complement of locks intact. It's a high-trust society. Tissue gets damaged, surviving cells are told to proliferate, and they behave.

Old bodies are not high-trust. Somatic evolution has been running for decades. In aged sun-exposed skin, [over a quarter](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaa6806) of cells carry at least one driver mutation in a known cancer gene — not enough for a tumor on its own, but enough that they're no longer fully cooperating. Clonal hematopoiesis — one mutant stem cell gradually taking over blood production — turns up in the majority of people over 70. 

Mosaicism is most obvious in tissues that divide rapidly — skin, intestinal lining, blood — but it extends even to the brain: the brain's support cells (glia, endothelial cells, microglia) do divide, and they accumulate mutations like everything else. The body is no longer a uniform population of cooperating cells. It's competing lineages carrying different mutations.

The cells with three out of five tumor suppressor locks already broken get another shot at the fourth every time they divide. Option 1: keep greenlighting proliferation to repair tissue damage, accepting the mounting cancer risk. Option 2: enter a coup-proof lockdown — trigger senescence, raise inflammatory baselines, suppress regeneration — which holds the mosaic in check but causes the frailty of old age. Senescent cells don't just stop dividing. They start pumping out inflammatory cytokines — IL-6, IL-8, TNF-alpha, matrix metalloproteinases — a cocktail called the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, or SASP. The SASP signals neighboring cells to stop dividing too, recruits immune cells to clear precancerous clones, and remodels the extracellular matrix to wall off suspicious patches of tissue. Useful if you have a few senescent cells. When half the tissue is senescent, you get chronic inflammation, fibrosis, muscle wasting, brittle bones, slow healing, declining immunity.

This isn't just a cancer-vs-Alzheimer's tradeoff, either. Clonal hematopoiesis — mutant stem cells taking over blood production, most commonly through TET2 or DNMT3A mutations — doubles the risk of coronary heart disease, probably through the same inflammatory pathway. Suppressing somatic mosaicism causes cardiovascular disease, frailty, neurodegeneration, and likely a dozen other things we file under "aging." Cancer avoidance is expensive. (This is part of why senolytics — drugs that selectively kill senescent cells — are being tested as anti-aging therapeutics. If the senescent cells are the ones paying the cost, clearing them might loosen the tradeoff. Dasatinib plus quercetin trials are underway. The theory is real even if the drugs aren't there yet.)

If you die of cancer at 60, your body leaned toward option 1. Degenerative disease at 80, option 2. 110, you split the difference and got lucky. Evolutionary biologists have a name for this: antagonistic pleiotropy. Genes that help you survive to reproduction can hurt you later because natural selection stops caring once you've passed on your DNA. This "aging as tumor suppression" framework crops up periodically in biogerontology, usually after the latest crop of anti-aging therapeutics disappoints. It's far from consensus.

You'd think the brain would be exempt from this tradeoff. Neurons don't divide. You basically can't get neuron-derived brain cancer (gliomas come from glial cells). The proliferation-vs-lockdown choice shouldn't directly touch them. Why would Alzheimer's be part of this story?

Because the brain isn't sealed off from a body at war with cheater cells in every other tissue. The blood-brain barrier gets leakier with age — tight junctions loosen, transport proteins change expression — and IL-6 and TNF-alpha from the SASP cross into the CNS and activate microglia. Neuroinflammation driven by peripheral immune signaling is one of the less controversial mechanisms in Alzheimer's pathology. The extracellular matrix stiffening that encapsulates nascent tumors also solidifies the matrix around amyloid aggregates in the brain. Brain degeneration could partly be collateral from the body fighting somatic mosaicism in gut, bone marrow, and skin. The neurons aren't in the line of fire. They're in the building next to it.

And when a tumor breaks through containment, it produces matrix metalloproteinases, cathepsins, and cystatin C to dissolve the tissue barriers in its way. The same chemicals loosen the rigid matrix that traps amyloid plaques in the brain. The invading army's battle engineers, repairing bridges the defending country blew up itself.

For thirty years, cystatin C's role in the brain seemed clear. It's the most abundant cysteine protease inhibitor in cerebrospinal fluid. Neurobiologists noticed in the 1990s that it accumulates around amyloid plaques. [Subsequent work](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnmol.2012.00079) showed it binds amyloid-beta directly, slowing aggregation into fibrils. The binding sites were mapped. CSF levels track with AD severity. Overexpression in mouse models reduced plaque burden. Everything pointed the same direction: cystatin C physically prevents amyloid from clumping.

The L68Q variant confirmed the picture from the other side — cystatin C itself misfolds and deposits in brain blood vessels, producing hereditary cerebral amyloid angiopathy. Normal cystatin C prevents clumping. Mutant cystatin C becomes the clump. If you'd asked anyone in the field before 2026, that was the answer.

The Li et al. paper broke it. They implanted multiple tumor types in AD model mice — Lewis lung carcinoma, B16 melanoma, MC38 colorectal — and saw plaque reduction across all of them. Then they screened the tumor secretome for what was doing it. Among hundreds of secreted proteins, cystatin C was the one that, when knocked out of tumor cells, eliminated the anti-plaque effect. And the mechanism wasn't amyloid binding — it was [TREM2](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2015.01.049) activation on microglia. Active immune recruitment, not passive chaperoning. The thirty-year literature on cystatin C and amyloid had the wrong mechanism. And the effect didn't require tumors — Li et al. injected purified recombinant cystatin C into AD mice without any cancer, and got the same plaque clearance. The tumor was the delivery vehicle, not the point.

TREM2 only entered the Alzheimer's picture in [2013](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1211103), when two independent groups reported that the R47H variant roughly triples the risk of late-onset AD. The deCODE Genetics team found it because they'd sequenced most of Iceland's population — a small, genetically homogeneous country where rare variants are easier to spot. University College London found it independently through exome sequencing. One of the strongest single-gene risk factors after APOE4. R47H [impairs TREM2's ability](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2015.01.049) to sense lipids on damaged cell membranes — which is normally how microglia know to cluster around debris and clear it. In AD brains, TREM2 is what tells microglia to surround amyloid plaques and prevent them from spreading. R47H microglia don't cluster. The plaques grow unchecked. This was a genetics finding. It had nothing to do with cancer, cystatin C, or the epidemiological inverse correlation.

But when Li et al. knocked in R47H, tumor-derived cystatin C stopped clearing plaques. The microglia couldn't respond. Same result with L68Q cystatin C — the misfolded version couldn't activate TREM2 either. Two mutations, on different chromosomes, in different genes, identified by completely unrelated research programs, converging on the same signaling chain from opposite ends. One breaks the receiver. The other breaks the signal. If either is disrupted, the pathway from peripheral tumor to brain plaque clearance goes dead. That kind of convergence from independent genetics is not what you'd expect if the cancer-Alzheimer's connection were just epidemiological noise.

So tumor-secreted cystatin C activates brain microglia to clear amyloid, via TREM2. But why would a tumor produce cystatin C?

If it's random metabolic byproduct, the brain effect is a lucky coincidence. A [2025 paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41392-025-02462-x) found it's not random. Tumors produce oligomeric cystatin C to suppress the immune cells trying to kill them — it binds LILRB2 and LILRB5, inhibitory receptors on myeloid cells. Delete the gene and tumors shrink. Overexpress it and cancer accelerates. It's not waste product. It's a weapon.

The Zhang paper found that it's specifically oligomeric cystatin C that binds the inhibitory LILRB receptors and suppresses peripheral immunity. The monomeric form doesn't. Li et al. showed monomeric cystatin C is what activates TREM2. Same protein, different folding states, different receptors, opposite effects. Both forms are in circulation.

TREM2 agonist antibodies are already in clinical trials from Denali Therapeutics and others. A pharmaceutical agonist could activate microglial plaque clearance without the immune suppression side, because it wouldn't touch LILRB2/LILRB5. Cystatin C itself would be a terrible drug — you'd be suppressing anti-tumor immunity to clear amyloid. But the target is already being pursued.

None of this means cancer is neuroprotective in any therapeutically useful sense. Cystatin C is one molecule in a library of thousands that tumors secrete, and mouse models overexpressing a single protein at defined concentrations don't map onto real human cancers.

But the molecular connection is real. Alzheimer's, under this lens, is collateral damage from the body's strategy for not dying of cancer first. When containment works — lockdown, inflammation, matrix stiffening — cancer stays in check but amyloid accumulates unbothered. When a tumor breaks through and secretes cystatin C to evade immune attack, it incidentally activates the microglial pathway that clears plaques. Break the pathway (TREM2 R47H) and you lose both — the anti-cancer function evolution intended and the neuroprotective one it didn't. The inverse correlation falls straight out of the tradeoff.
