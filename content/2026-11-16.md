AI: For a mainstream **New Mechanist**, **"their optic gland excretes hormones causing fasting and death" is completely sufficient**—*provided* you are clear about which phenomenon you are trying to explain.

In fact, one of the defining moves of the New Mechanism movement (launched around the turn of the millennium by **Peter Machamer, Lindley Darden, Carl Craver [MDC 2000]**, **William Bechtel**, and **Stuart Glennan**) was to break Ernst Mayr’s classic *proximate vs. ultimate* divide down into more fine-grained explanatory norms. Once you look inside the movement, however, philosophers divide sharply into **four distinct camps** over whether—and how—evolutionary/ecological history enters a mechanistic explanation.

---

### 1. The Orthodoxy: Keep Them Separate (Craver, Darden, Kaplan, Bechtel, Maley & Piccinini)

The classic New Mechanism school was built primarily to explain molecular biology, cell biology, and neuroscience. Its core instinct is that **the boundaries of a mechanism are fixed strictly by the explanandum phenomenon**. 

Drawing on **Wesley Salmon (1984)**, **Carl Craver (2001, 2007, 2013)** argues that causal-mechanical explanation has three distinct aspects:
1. **Constitutive:** What organized parts and activities *inside* the system produce or realize the phenomenon right now? (For octopus semelparity: after egg-laying, the optic gland alters cholesterol metabolism and upregulates pregnane steroids, 7-dehydrocholesterol, and bile acid intermediates, shutting down feeding and triggering maternal brooding and senescence).
2. **Contextual:** How does this mechanism sit inside a larger containing mechanism? (e.g., how the mother's fasting/guarding behavior oxygenates and protects the egg clutch).
3. **Etiological:** What *prior causes* brought the mechanism or event into existence? (This is where developmental history and evolutionary selection pressures live.)

If your question is *"How does an individual female octopus undergo post-reproductive self-destruction?"*, Craver and **David Kaplan (2020, *"Are More Details Better?"*)** argue that a constitutive explanation is complete when it includes *all and only* the factors constitutively relevant to that phenomenon. Including *"their ecological niche favors semelparity because of high adult predation or cannibalism"* in the molecular mechanism would be a **category mistake**—conflating a *constitutive* question with an *etiological* (evolutionary) one.

To avoid smuggling evolutionary history into physiology, this camp also uses **ahistorical theories of biological function**:
* **Craver (2001, 2013)** adopts a perspectivalist version of **Robert Cummins’s (1975)** *causal-role* theory of function: calling hormone secretion the "function" of the optic gland is simply a researcher’s way of picking out which higher-level capacity (brooding/senescence) they want to decompose, without needing to cite natural selection.
* **Corey Maley and Gualtiero Piccinini (2017)** go further, arguing that biological mechanisms have *objective, non-historical* teleological functions grounded solely in their *current* causal contributions to an organism's survival or reproduction—explicitly rejecting evolutionary history as causally impotent in the here-and-now and often epistemically inaccessible.
* **William Bechtel** (e.g., **2009, 2011; Bechtel & Bich 2024**) emphasizes *control mechanisms* and biological autonomy. For Bechtel, evolution *crafted* these regulatory architectures over deep time (which explains why neuroendocrine mechanisms are conserved across species), but you explain how the octopus behaves by modeling its present-day control architecture, not its ancestral ecology.

---

### 2. Wait—Is Natural Selection Even a "Mechanism"? (Skipper & Millstein vs. Glennan, Barros, Illari & Williamson)

Even if you pivot to the evolutionary question (*"Why are octopuses semelparous?"*), New Mechanists fight bitterly over whether evolutionary explanations are mechanistic at all:

* **The Skeptics:** **Robert Skipper and Roberta Millstein (2005)** famously argued that **natural selection is *not* a mechanism** in either the MDC (2000) or early Glennan (1996/2002) sense. Molecular mechanisms like the optic gland have stable spatial organization, fixed component parts (glands, steroid molecules, receptors), and continuous productive interactions. Natural selection operates over changing populations, lacks stable physical boundaries or fixed architecture, and works statistically/stochastically. **Joyce Havstad (2011)** and **Saúl Pérez-González and Víctor Luque (2019)** defend and sharpen this critique.
* **The Expanders:** To save evolutionary biology for New Mechanism, **D. Benjamin Barros (2008)** argued natural selection *is* a two-level stochastic mechanism. **Phyllis Illari and Jon Williamson (2010, 2012)** deliberately formulated a stripped-down, "minimal" definition of mechanism so that *both* protein synthesis and natural selection count. **John Matthewson and Brett Calcott (2011)** similarly showed how population-level ecological and evolutionary dynamics can be modeled mechanistically.
* **Stuart Glennan’s Split (2010, 2017):** In *The New Mechanical Philosophy*, **Stuart Glennan** distinguishes **system mechanisms** (stable, recurring, organized systems—like the octopus's optic gland pathway) from **ephemeral mechanisms** (one-off, contingent historical causal chains). Under Glennan’s taxonomy, *both* the hormone cascade and the historical sequence of niche selection pressures are mechanisms, just fundamentally different kinds. 

Meanwhile, **Craver and Darden (2013, *In Search of Mechanisms*)** mostly sidestepped the debate over whether natural selection fits MDC's strict criteria, calling it largely terminological and arguing that *"a perhaps more fruitful topic is the evolution of mechanisms themselves"*.

---

### 3. The Historical Pushback: Why "Hormone Alone" Isn't Quite Enough (Justin Garson)

One major dissenter *within* the mechanism/function literature directly attacks the ahistorical orthodoxy: **Justin Garson (2013; 2023, *"Putting History Back into Mechanisms"*)**.

Garson argues that biologists don't use "mechanism" in a "thin" sense (any arbitrary chain of causes and effects). They use a "thick," **functional sense of mechanism**, where mechanisms have *proper functions* and can malfunction or break. Drawing on the **selected-effects** tradition (**Larry Wright, Ruth Millikan, Karen Neander**), Garson argues that proper functions are inherently historical—and therefore **biological mechanisms are historical entities**.

In fact, Garson (2023) explicitly targets **Ernst Mayr’s proximate/ultimate distinction** as resting on an unstable foundation. Apply his argument to the octopus:
* Imagine an octopus develops a random pituitary tumor that leaks a toxin causing it to stop eating and die right after laying eggs. Molecularly, there is a clear causal cascade from gland to death.
* Yet no biologist calls that tumor a *"mechanism for semelparity"*—it is a fatal pathology.
* What makes the *actual* optic gland's steroid surge a **mechanism for semelparity** rather than an endocrine disease? Only the fact that it was **historically selected for** in the octopus's evolutionary/ecological niche!

For Garson, then, you can describe the *causal chain* in purely molecular terms, **but you cannot even individuate it *as* a mechanism for semelparity without implicit reference to its evolutionary/ecological history**. (In a follow-up paper, **Harriet Fagerberg and Justin Garson [2024]** refine this: the *proper function* itself is proximal—e.g., suppressing feeding/triggering brooding—rather than a distal ecological payoff, even though what *confers* that function is evolutionary selection history.)

---

### 4. Evo-Devo & Ecological Integrationists: Bridging the Two (Calcott, Brigandt, Kaiser & Trappes, Villegas, Potochnik, Levy)

Finally, a wave of philosophers working on **evo-devo** and **behavioral ecology** argue that while you shouldn't mash molecular and ecological factors into a single causal box, keeping them strictly segregated misses how biology actually explains things:

* **Brett Calcott (2009, 2013):** In *"Lineage Explanations"* and *"Why how and why aren't enough"*, Calcott critiques Mayr’s binary. If you want to explain how an iteroparous cephalopod ancestor (like *Nautilus*, or an iteroparous octopus like *Octopus chierchiae*) evolved into a semelparous one (*Octopus bimaculoides*), citing ecological selection pressure alone isn't enough, and citing the optic gland alone isn't enough. You need a **lineage explanation**—a step-by-step historical account showing how the **molecular/developmental mechanisms themselves** were incrementally rewired over evolutionary time without breaking organismal viability.
* **Ingo Brigandt (2015)** and **Alan Love:** They argue that evo-devo exposes the limits of stereotypical New Mechanism (fixed parts, linear sequences within one organism). Molecular and evolutionary explanations don't collapse into one model; instead, biology achieves **explanatory integration** by coordinating distinct mechanistic and evolutionary models around a shared "problem agenda".
* **Kevin Laland, Kim Sterelny, Tobias Uller et al. (2011)** and **Cristina Villegas (2024):** They replace Mayr's one-way divide with **reciprocal causation**. Proximate molecular and developmental mechanisms actively bias, constrain, and reshape the very ecological selection pressures acting on the species.
* **Marie Kaiser and Rose Trappes (2023; Trappes et al. 2022):** Extending New Mechanism into ecology, they model **$NC^3$ mechanisms** (niche construction, niche choice, niche conformance). An octopus's neuroendocrine brooding cascade directly alters its ecological interactions (den choice, predator exposure, adult-offspring competition), physically coupling the individual molecular mechanism to the ecological selective regime.
* **Arnon Levy (2013)** and **Angela Potochnik (2017, 2020):** They stress **abstraction and explanatory aims**. Evolutionary life-history models *deliberately abstract away* from optic gland steroids to show why semelparity pays off across diverse taxa (salmon, octopuses, agave plants) in certain ecological niches; molecular models *deliberately abstract away* from ecological niches to find biochemical intervention targets. Neither is a "failed" or "incomplete" version of the other.

### Bottom Line
If you hand a New Mechanist the octopus example:
* **Craver, Kaplan, Bechtel, and Maley & Piccinini** will say: *"The optic gland hormone cascade is completely enough to explain **how** the octopus dies after reproducing. Leave the ecological niche to the evolutionary biologists—that's a different explanandum."*
* **Justin Garson** will say: *"The hormones tell you the causal pathway, sure, but without the ecological selection history, you can't even tell whether that gland is a **mechanism for semelparity** or a weird fatal tumor."*
* **Calcott, Brigandt, Kaiser & Trappes, and Villegas** will say: *"Both explanations are distinct, but if you want to understand how semelparity actually **evolved** or how the organism interacts with its niche, you have to trace how the hormonal mechanism and the ecological pressure co-shaped each other."*

### 1. *"All a cell needs for the cell cycle is one cyclin and one CDK"*
**Mostly true (with important caveats).**

* **Where you are spot-on:**
  * **Fission yeast (*S. pombe*):** In a famous synthetic biology experiment, Coudreuse & Nurse (2010) deleted all native cell-cycle cyclins and the sole cell-cycle CDK (*cdc2*) and replaced them with **a single engineered fusion protein** (one B-type cyclin, Cdc13, fused to Cdc2). That single cyclin–CDK module drove the entire mitotic cell cycle in the right order—and a follow-up study showed that this same single complex can even drive **meiosis**.
  * **How one pair pulls it off:** It works via the **quantitative threshold model**. S-phase (DNA replication) substrates are easy to phosphorylate and trigger at a *low* level of CDK activity, whereas M-phase (mitosis) substrates require a *high* level of CDK activity. As the single cyclin–CDK accumulates, it crosses the S-phase threshold and then the M-phase threshold in sequence, before being destroyed at mitotic exit.
  * **Early frog embryos:** Murray & Kirschner (1989) similarly showed that oscillating synthesis and destruction of a single cyclin (Cyclin B) drives rapid early embryonic cell cycles in frog egg extracts.
  * **Mammals (at least on the CDK side):** Santamaría et al. (2007) knocked out *all* interphase CDKs in mice (*Cdk2, Cdk3, Cdk4, Cdk6*) and found that **Cdk1 alone** can bind all cyclins, phosphorylate Rb, and drive the cell cycle in mouse fibroblasts and through mid-gestation organogenesis (embryos survive until ~E13.5–E15.5).
* **The caveats:**
  * **You still need the rest of the core oscillator:** One cyclin + one CDK cannot cycle by themselves. You still need the **APC/C** (to destroy the cyclin and reset the cycle), **phosphatases** (like PP2A, which oppose CDK and establish those activity thresholds), and a CDK-activating kinase (**CAK**).
  * **In mammals and budding yeast, one *cyclin* isn't enough in vivo:** Even Cdk1-only mice still needed multiple cyclins (D, E, A, B) and still died mid-gestation from severe hematopoietic and heart proliferation defects. Knocking out Cyclin A2 or Cyclin B1 alone causes early embryonic death. Similarly, in budding yeast (*S. cerevisiae*), forcing a single B-type cyclin to run the whole cycle fails to support polarized growth and bud formation.

### 2. *"Metazoans have evolved more than one solely for regulatory reasons"*
**This is the weakest part of the statement—it’s chronologically backwards and too narrow.**

* **Single-celled organisms *already* have many cyclins and CDKs:**
  * You do not need a large, multicellular body to evolve multiple cyclins/CDKs. Even single-celled **yeasts** have a crowd of them: *S. pombe* has 11 cyclins (4 dedicated to the mitotic cell cycle) and 7 CDKs, while *S. cerevisiae* has **15 cyclins (9 just for the cell cycle)** and 6 CDKs.
  * Comparative phylogenomics shows that **the Last Eukaryotic Common Ancestor (LECA)** and unicellular animal relatives (like choanoflagellates and *Capsaspora*) *already* possessed multiple CDK subfamilies and multiple cell-cycle cyclins (Cyclins A, B, and E) long before animals appeared.
  * Even a single-celled organism needs multiple cyclins to coordinate division with **nutrient availability, cell size, mating signals, specialized cell shape/budding, DNA damage, and meiosis**.
* **Plants expanded their toolkit massively too:**
  * *Arabidopsis* has ~**49+ cyclins** and plant-specific CDKs (**CDKB**, alongside CDKA). Plants don't get metastatic cancers [rigid cell walls lock cells in place], yet multicellular plant development, indeterminate meristem growth, endoreduplication, and environmental plasticity still drove a massive expansion of cyclins and CDKs.
* **It isn't *solely* regulatory—there is biochemical specialization and non-cell-cycle work:**
  * **Most human CDKs don't run the cell cycle at all:** Out of ~20 CDKs and ~29 cyclins in humans, a huge fraction control **RNA Polymerase II transcription and RNA processing** (CDK7, CDK8/19, CDK9, CDK12, CDK13) or **post-mitotic neuronal function** (CDK5).
  * **Substrate specificity (the "qualitative" model):** Cyclins aren't just interchangeable on/off switches; they carry **substrate-docking pockets** (like the hydrophobic patch) that physically recruit different sets of target proteins at different speeds. Likewise, **CDK4 and CDK6** are structurally distinct from CDK1/2 and are narrowly specialized to phosphorylate **Rb-family proteins** rather than general mitotic targets.
  * **Specialized cell cycles:** Mammals also rely on dedicated complexes for **meiosis** (Cdk2, Cyclin A1, Cyclin B3) and **endoreduplication** (e.g., Cyclin E in placental trophoblast giant cells).

### 3. *"Our large long-living bodies require smart regulation to prevent cancers while at the same time allowing regeneration"*
**Spot-on for the specific innovation that *did* emerge with complex animals: G1 control.**

While animals didn't invent *multiple* cyclins and CDKs from scratch, phylogenetic studies (e.g., Cao et al., 2014) show that **eumetazoans uniquely assembled and locked in the CDK4/6–Cyclin D module** right alongside the evolution of differentiated somatic tissues (it is present in placozoans like *Trichoplax* and cnidarians, but absent in sponges, choanoflagellates, and fungi).

Your evolutionary reasoning hits the nail on the head for **why that G1 machinery matters**:
* Single-celled organisms generally default to dividing whenever nutrients and size allow.
* A large, long-lived animal faces the opposite problem: **the vast majority of cells must remain parked in quiescence (G0) or terminal differentiation**, dividing *only* when external growth factors/tissue-damage cues give permission, and halting immediately upon DNA damage or oncogenic stress.
* **Cyclin D–CDK4/6** (paired with the **Rb/E2F** restriction point and animal-expanded CDK inhibitors like **p16^INK4a** and **p21/p27**) is the exact molecular gate that mediates that tug-of-war between **tissue regeneration** and **cancer suppression**. Indeed, mice lacking D-type cyclins have reduced susceptibility to oncogenic transformation, and the Cyclin D–CDK4/6–p16–Rb axis is disrupted in a huge fraction of human cancers.
