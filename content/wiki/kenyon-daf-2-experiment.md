# Kenyon daf-2 experiment

The **Kenyon daf-2 experiment** was a 1993 genetic study in the nematode *Caenorhabditis elegans* that showed a single mutation can double adult lifespan. Cynthia Kenyon's laboratory found that mutations in *daf-2* let fertile, active adult hermaphrodites live more than twice as long as wild-type animals, and that the effect required a second gene, *daf-16*. The result made aging a genetically regulated process, and it opened the insulin/IGF-1 signaling pathway as a conserved control point for longevity.[^1][^2][^3]

## Background

*C. elegans* exits the reproductive life cycle and enters the dauer diapause stage when it senses crowding and starvation. Dauer larvae are long-lived and stress-resistant, and genetic screens had identified *daf-2* and *daf-16* among the genes that control the decision to enter and leave that stage.[^2][^3] Because dauer larvae combine developmental arrest with extended survival, the field treated their longevity as a consequence of the arrest. Kenyon's group asked whether the lifespan increase could be separated from the developmental arrest, and whether it operated in animals that developed normally.

## Design

The experiment used *C. elegans* genetics to measure the effect of *daf-2* on adult lifespan. Kenyon's group raised animals carrying *daf-2* mutations at permissive temperatures, where the worms developed into fertile adults, and recorded survival across the adult lifespan. They then combined each *daf-2* mutation with a *daf-16* mutation to test whether the longevity effect depended on *daf-16*. Lifespan was scored as the number of days the animals survived as adults, with reproduction, movement, and morphology tracked alongside survival.

## Result

Animals carrying *daf-2* mutations lived more than twice as long as wild type, the largest lifespan extension reported in any organism at the time. The long-lived animals stayed fertile and active, so their longevity reflected a regulated extension of the normal adult lifespan. Removing *daf-16* suppressed the *daf-2* effect, which placed *daf-16* downstream of *daf-2* in the same pathway. Both genes also influenced dauer formation, which connected the developmental and longevity phenotypes to one signaling system. The authors proposed that dauer longevity itself came from a regulated lifespan-extension mechanism that the adult *daf-2* mutants also used.[^1]

## What it established

The study produced the first clear evidence that a single genetic change can lengthen adult life:

- *daf-2* mutations more than double adult lifespan in *C. elegans*.
- The effect requires *daf-16*, so the two genes act in one pathway.
- Longevity can be uncoupled from developmental arrest.

Later work identified the molecular parts. *daf-2* encodes an insulin-receptor-family protein, and *daf-16* encodes a forkhead transcription factor that the DAF-2 pathway restrains.[^2][^3] The pathway's components are conserved, with the mammalian counterparts being [[igf-1|IGF-1]] signaling and the [[foxo3-foxo3-o43524|FOXO3]] transcription factor, and IGF-1 signaling has since been tied to longevity in mice and to human variation. The experiment turned aging into a problem of signal transduction, which is the framing that the [[baker-ink-attac-experiment|Baker INK-ATTAC experiment]] and other intervention studies inherited.

## Limits and later work

The result came from one nematode species, and it measured lifespan under laboratory conditions with a fixed food supply and temperature. The *daf-2* pathway regulates metabolism, dauer entry, and stress resistance together, so the experiment tied longevity to a broad physiological switch that acts across several processes. Whether the pathway's contribution to lifespan in worms extends to mammals with longer developmental programs and different ecology is a separate question that later mouse and human genetics addressed. The [[hayflick-moorhead-experiment|Hayflick–Moorhead experiment]] had shown that individual cell lineages have a division limit; the *daf-2* result showed that a whole organism's lifespan can be reset by a regulatory mutation.
