# Weismann Barrier

**Weismann barrier** is the principle that hereditary information flows in one direction: from the germline — the cells that produce eggs and sperm — into the rest of the body, never from a body cell back into an egg or sperm. The practical consequence is that a change a body acquires during its lifetime, such as a mutation in a muscle or skin cell, is not inherited by its offspring, because those cells never reach an egg or sperm. There is no wall or membrane involved; the "barrier" is the direction of flow itself.[^1]

![[weismann-barrier-germ-plasm.png|Diagram of Weismann's germ-plasm theory: the vertical line of hereditary material continues unbroken from generation to generation, while each body branches off, develops, and dies. Wikimedia Commons.]]

The diagram shows the two roles the one-way flow depends on. The germ-plasm is the unbroken thread running from generation to generation. Each body grows from it as a side branch that inherits the germ-plasm it is made from but does not feed anything back into the thread. Somatic mutations are confined to the body they arise in; only germline mutations reach the next generation.

## History

The accepted account of heredity before Weismann was Charles Darwin's theory of pangenesis: changes anywhere in the body were thought to be carried to the reproductive organs by circulating particles, so that experience could be stamped into heredity. The alternative that came to be called the Weismann barrier was not new in outline. The "continuity of germ-plasm" had already been "present to Darwin's mind as a logically possible alternative to the one which he adopted in his theory of pangenesis". Francis Galton, Darwin's cousin, published a theory of heredity in the early 1870s that answered the question of acquired characters "in almost exactly the same manner as Weismann did about ten years later". George John Romanes, writing in the generation after Darwin's death, reported both facts.[^2]

Weismann published his full doctrine in German in 1892 as *Das Keimplasma*, translated into English in 1893 as *The Germ-Plasm: A Theory of Heredity*. The barrier doctrine was one theory among several advanced under his name, and it concerned only the separation of the germline from the body: heredity is carried in a separate, continuous germ-plasm, and the body is rebuilt from that germ-plasm in each generation.[^1]

Romanes's 1893 book, *An Examination of Weismannism*, gave the doctrine the name "Weismannism" by which contemporaries knew it. Romanes insisted that the "inheritance or non-inheritance of acquired characters" was "a question of fact", and one "clearly quite distinct from that of any theories which may be framed upon either side of it" — the question itself "was not raised by Weismann". The challenge he recorded was that somatic tissue might influence the germ: grafting experiments appeared to show that "the somatic-tissues of an organism A, by being merely grafted on those of organism B, can so affect the germinal elements of B as to cause their offspring to resemble A" — evidence, he noted, that had been "disregarded both by Weismann and his followers".[^3]

## Definition

The precise claim concerns the direction of hereditary information flow. Hereditary information is copied from germline to soma, never from soma to germline. In the terms of a formal model of differentiation, "the developmental strategy must allow germ-role cells to give rise to soma-role cells" and "must forbid soma-role cells to give rise to germ-role cells".[^4]

Weismann's own statement, from the preface of the 1893 book: heredity rests on "a special organised and living hereditary substance, which in all multicellular organisms, unlike the substance composing the perishable body of the individual, is transmitted from generation to generation". He called this "the theory of the continuity of the germ-plasm".[^1]

The barrier is narrower than the catchphrase "acquired characteristics are not inherited". The doctrine does not assert that nothing an organism undergoes can reach its descendants. It asserts that body and heredity are two distinct questions, that experience changes the body directly, and that the germline — not the body — is the link to the next generation. Romanes drew exactly this separation between the two questions.[^2]

## Exceptions

The strong one-way form applies to organisms with a true, early-segregated germline. Two large biological groups violate it.

**Plants** are not bound by a one-way flow. "Exceptions from irreversible somatic differentiation are widespread in plants", and such reversals "are even known in simpler metazoans among cnidarians".[^5] In plants the gametes descend from somatic cell lines: a mutation that appears in a meristem becomes part of the flowering lineage and can enter the seeds. The contrast with animals with a sequestered germ line is sharp: somatic-cell mutants "have little hope for a future of their own", whereas a plant mutant can pass on precisely when it occurs in a dividing meristematic tissue.[^6]

**Basal metazoans** and other lineages without an early-segregated germ line keep pluripotent stem cells "scattered" through the adult body — the source lists "plant meristems, stem cells in cnidarians, circulating cells in colonial ascidians" — and some later "develop into germ cells". These lineages show that germ-line sequestration is "not an essential defense for the evolution of multicellular differentiation, even in lineages with motile cells".[^8]

The barrier is thus a matter of timing as much as kind. A defector can be transmitted only if it strikes before a host sequesters its germ line, arises "in a germ line or meristem", or invades an organism that does not sequester one at all.[^7]

## Evolution of the barrier

The strict one-way separation carries a cost, and models describe when selection pays it. Irreversible somatic differentiation is favored when three conditions hold: costly cell differentiation; a few vegetative cells that significantly improve the organism's performance; and a large enough organism size.[^9] Under those conditions the soma works as a disposable manufacturing layer, and closing its input — the soma's mutations never re-enter the germline — keeps the lineage from passing defector mutations that could otherwise drag the next generation into the errors of a single body.[^6]

## Consequences

The germline–soma split shaped two problems in biology. First, in the history of ideas: "After the publication of Darwin's Origin of Species, but before the general acceptance of Weismann's views, problems of evolution and development were inextricably bound up with one another. One consequence of Weismann's concept of the separation of germ line and soma was to make it possible to understand genetics, and hence evolution, without understanding development."[^10]

Second, it frames the modern account of cancer as somatic evolution within a single body: "genetic and epigenetic instability generating fitness variation among the cells of a body". Because the barrier keeps somatic mutations out of the germline, that evolution stays within the lifetime of the body; the somatic events it selects are never handed to the next generation.[^11]
