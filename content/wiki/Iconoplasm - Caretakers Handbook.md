# Iconoplasm - Caretaker's Handbook


## Timescales

A useful anchor is about a 10-hour cell cycle. There is no single clean “fastest human cell” record because individual-cycle minima, sustained population doubling, transformed cells, and primary cells give different answers. Human embryonic stem cells are usually around 15–16 hours, while individual human hTERT-immortalized fibroblasts have been observed near 10–12 hours. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8080833/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Maintenance of genome integrity and active homologous recombination in embryonic stem cells - PMC")) I will use **10 hours → 100 years**, because it gives an especially intuitive scale.

That stretches time by **87,660-fold**.

The surprising conversion is:

|--:|--:|
|1 picosecond|88 nanoseconds|
|1 nanosecond|88 microseconds|
|1 microsecond|0.088 seconds|
|1 millisecond|1.46 minutes|
|10 milliseconds|14.6 minutes|
|100 milliseconds|2.44 hours|
|**1 second**|**24.35 hours**|
|10 seconds|10.1 days|
|**1 minute**|**60.9 days**|
|5 minutes|10 months|
|10 minutes|1.67 years|
|30 minutes|5 years|
|**1 hour**|**10 years**|
|5 hours|50 years|
|**10 hours**|**100 years**|
|24 hours|240 years|

So the easiest way to think about this universe is: **one molecular-biological second is approximately one scaled day**.

The extremely fast end is perhaps the strangest. Molecular motion still looks fast even when a cell division has been turned into an entire human lifetime. A globular protein tumbles roughly a million times per second, and small metabolites can cross cellular distances in fractions of a second. ([NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26838/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Catalysis and the Use of Energy by Cells - Molecular Biology of the Cell - NCBI Bookshelf")) Thus a protein is still jittering and reorienting on approximately **human reaction-time scales** in this stretched world, while substrate diffusion takes hours.

Enzymology moves into an especially intuitive regime. Molecular Biology of the Cell gives roughly 1,000 catalytic events per second for a typical fast enzyme. ([NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26838/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Catalysis and the Use of Energy by Cells - Molecular Biology of the Cell - NCBI Bookshelf")) At our scale, one catalytic turnover every millisecond means **one reaction every minute and a half**. An enzyme doing 10 reactions per second performs one roughly every **2.4 hours**. An enzyme doing one reaction per second performs one every **day**. A metabolic pathway therefore resembles a factory whose individual machines act over minutes to days.

Gene expression becomes extraordinarily slow. Mammalian transcription proceeds on the order of tens of nucleotides per second, while translation is around ten amino acids per second. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11000238/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Living Cell as a Self-Synchronized Chemical Reactor - PMC")) A ribosome making a 500-amino-acid protein consequently spends around 50 real seconds on elongation, which becomes about **51 scaled days**. A 10-minute transcription event takes **1.7 years**. Making a new protein in response to transcriptional activation feels less like sending a biochemical message and more like commissioning infrastructure.

That also changes the intuitive status of signaling. A small molecule crossing about 10 μm in 0.2 seconds becomes a five-hour trip. ([NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26838/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Catalysis and the Use of Energy by Cells - Molecular Biology of the Cell - NCBI Bookshelf")) A phosphorylation that lasts seconds persists for days. A regulatory protein surviving ten minutes survives for 1.7 years. Some intracellular regulatory proteins genuinely have half-lives below ten minutes, precisely because cells need those concentrations to change rapidly. ([NCBI](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26813/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "General Principles of Cell Communication - Molecular Biology of the Cell - NCBI Bookshelf"))

The oscillators become particularly striking. TNF-driven NF-κB oscillations around every 90 minutes become approximately **15-year cycles**. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3942427/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "High-Throughput Analysis of NF-κB Dynamics in Single Cells Reveals Basal Nuclear Localization of NF-κB and Spontaneous Activation of Oscillations - PMC")) DNA-damage-induced p53 pulses, whose characteristic period is about 5.5 hours, arrive roughly **once every 55 years**. ([PubMed Central (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1681500/?utm_source=chatgpt.com "Oscillations and variability in the p53 system - PMC")) A cell completing one century-long cycle might therefore experience only about two canonical p53 oscillator periods during that entire “lifetime.”

And this gives a useful way of mentally partitioning molecular biology:

**Atomic physics** occupies nanoseconds through seconds.  
**Enzyme chemistry and channel gating** occupies minutes through hours.  
**Diffusion and proximal signaling** occupies hours through days.  
**Translation and sustained signaling** occupies months.  
**Transcriptional programs and trafficking** occupy years.  
**Cell-cycle regulatory dynamics** occupy decades.  
**A division** occupies a century.  
**Tissue turnover and organismal cell lifetimes** extend into millennia and millions of years.

There is one particularly nice consequence of choosing the 10-hour anchor: when reading a live-cell paper, you can almost mechanically replace **“second” with “day,” “minute” with “two months,” and “hour” with “decade.”** That preserves the enormous separation of timescales inside a cell much better than the usual animations, where diffusion, phosphorylation, transcription, protein synthesis, and division are all shown happening within a few seconds.
