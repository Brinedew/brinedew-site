# Mel Greaves

![Mel Greaves](../Attachments/mel-greaves.jpg)

Sir Melvyn ("Mel") Greaves (born 1941) is a British cancer biologist and professor at the Institute of Cancer Research in London, known for pioneering Darwinian [clonal evolution](clonal-evolution.md) and [cancer ecology](cancer-ecology.md) models of malignancy, particularly childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL).

## Career and honors

Greaves built his career in London immunology and leukemia biology, founding and directing the Centre for Evolution and Cancer at the Institute of Cancer Research (ICR) to unite evolutionary theory with experimental hematology. For his contributions to leukemia etiology and evolutionary oncology, he was elected a **Fellow of the Royal Society (FRS) in 2003**, received the Royal Society's **Royal Medal in 2017**, and was **knighted (Knight Bachelor)** in the 2019 New Year Honours for services to childhood leukemia research.

## Cancer as an evolutionary process

Greaves's theoretical synthesis treats tumors as evolving ecosystems governed by natural selection in microenvironmental niches: somatic mutations generate clonal diversity, while tissue architecture, infection/inflammation, and clinical chemotherapy filter which subclones survive. His book *Cancer: The Evolutionary Legacy* (2000) popularized Darwinian oncology by tracing human vulnerability to cancer not only to recent environmental exposures, but also to evolutionary mismatches and trade-offs rooted in human life history.

## Childhood leukemia and variegated clonal relapse

Greaves is best known for reconstructing the natural history of pediatric acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), especially subtypes carrying the *ETV6-RUNX1* translocation. Twin studies and neonatal blood-spot back-tracking showed that the *ETV6-RUNX1* fusion frequently arises prenatally *in utero*, establishing a covert pre-leukemic clone, while progression to clinical ALL requires secondary cooperating genetic hits in early childhood. His **"delayed infection" hypothesis** proposes that low early-life microbial exposure leaves immune networks dysregulated, so subsequent common infections trigger abnormal proliferative/inflammatory stress that promotes secondary mutations in predisposed pre-leukemic cells.

Analyzing genetic architecture at single-cell resolution (Anderson et al., *Nature*, 2011), Greaves and collaborators showed that *ETV6-RUNX1* ALL does not follow a simple single-track linear sweep; instead, leukemia-propagating cells exhibit intricate **genetic variegation**—multiple branching subclones with distinct secondary lesions coexisting before diagnosis and therapy. When patients relapse after cytotoxic treatment, recurrence frequently expands not from the most abundant clone at diagnosis, but from minor pre-existing subclones or ancestral cells carrying distinct combinations of survival lesions.
