# Clonal evolution

**Clonal evolution** is the evolutionary model of cancer progression in which a neoplasm functions as an adapting population of somatic cells descended from a single ancestor, within which heritable variation arises continuously and natural selection determines which subclones expand.[^1] Formulated by [Peter Nowell](peter-nowell.md) in 1976, the framework treats carcinogenesis as somatic Darwinian evolution inside host tissues.[^1]

## The Nowell model

Two coupled processes drive somatic progression:[^1]

- **Variation.** Genomic instability, replication errors, structural rearrangements, and environmental DNA damage continually generate genetically distinct subpopulations within a single lineage. [Mutation supply](replicative-credit.md) sets the rate at which the population samples new phenotypes.
- **Selection.** Microenvironmental conditions—nutrient scarcity, spatial crowding, [immune surveillance](immune-surveillance.md), and clinical therapy—determine which variants survive and outcompete neighbors. Genetic instability accelerates progression not because instability helps any single cell directly, but because it broadens the supply of selectable variants.

Tumorigenesis advances through sequential and overlapping clonal sweeps: a founder variant gains a proliferative edge, expands, acquires secondary genetic or epigenetic changes, and buds diverging subclones as local conditions shift.

## Trunk and branch mutations

Early models depicted neoplastic progression as a linear succession of mutations shared across every malignant cell. Multiregion sequencing overturned that linear picture. Sequencing spatially separated regions from primary and metastatic renal cell carcinomas revealed pervasive **branched evolution**:[^3]

- **Trunk mutations** arise early in the common ancestor of the neoplasm and appear in all sampled regions of the primary tumor and its metastases.
- **Branch mutations** accumulate later along spatially separated lineages, generating subclones with non-overlapping driver alterations—including independent convergent mutations in distinct regions of the same gene or pathway across one tumor.[^3]

Work led by [Mel Greaves](mel-greaves.md) documented an analogous branching ("variegated") architecture at single-cell resolution in childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia, demonstrating that competing subclones coexist prior to diagnosis and relapse.[^2] Single-needle biopsies therefore sample only a subset of terminal branches, risking false confidence that a branch alteration is present throughout the tumor.[^3]

## Clinical consequences

- **Target escape.** Drugs aimed solely at branch mutations eliminate only the subclone sitting on that limb, leaving trunk-only and parallel-branch lineages intact to repopulate the mass.[^3]
- **Therapy as selection.** Maximum-tolerated-dose regimens select sharply for resistant branches while eliminating sensitive competitors that restrained them, producing *competitive release*—a dynamic [adaptive therapy](adaptive-therapy.md) seeks to avoid.
- **Relapse from minor subclones.** Recurrent disease is frequently seeded not by the dominant pretreatment population, but by a minor pre-existing subclone or ancestral cell surviving therapeutic bottlenecks.[^2]

Clonal evolution forms the conceptual foundation for [cancer ecology](cancer-ecology.md) and evolutionary treatment strategies that manage malignancies as changing populations rather than static genotypes.
