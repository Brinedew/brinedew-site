# Clonal evolution

Clonal evolution is the model of cancer progression in which a neoplasm behaves as an evolving population of somatic cells descended from a single common ancestor, within which heritable variation arises continuously and natural selection determines which subclones expand. [Peter Nowell](peter-nowell.md) articulated the modern synthesis in his 1976 *Science* landmark *"The clonal evolution of tumor cell populations."*

## The 1976 Nowell model

Two processes drive somatic tumor progression:

- **Variation.** Genomic instability, replication errors, structural rearrangements, and environmental DNA damage generate genetically diverse subpopulations within a single lineage. [Mutation supply](replicative-credit.md) sets the rate at which a tissue explores phenotype space.
- **Selection.** Microenvironmental pressures — nutrient depletion, spatial crowding, host [immune surveillance](immune-surveillance.md), and therapy — determine which variants survive and outgrow competitors. Genetic instability accelerates evolution not because instability itself helps a single cell, but because it feeds a denser tail of selectable variants.

Progression proceeds through sequential and overlapping sweeps: a founder variant gains an early growth edge, expands, acquires secondary genetic or epigenetic lesions, and throws off diverging subclones as environments shift across space and time.

## Trunk and branch mutations: multiregion sequencing

For decades, linear schematic models depicted progression as a tidy ladder of mutations shared across every tumor cell. Multiregion DNA sequencing overturned that picture. In a pivotal study of primary and metastatic renal cell carcinoma, Gerlinger et al. (*The New England Journal of Medicine*, 2012) sequenced spatially separated regions from single tumors and revealed pervasive **branched evolution**:

- **Trunk mutations** arise early in the common ancestor of the neoplasm and remain present in *every* sampled region of the tumor and its metastases.
- **Branch mutations** accumulate later along spatially distinct evolutionary trajectories, producing spatially segregated subclones with non-overlapping driver alterations (even convergent mutations hitting different exons or alleles of the same pathway in separate parts of the same kidney mass).

The immediate diagnostic corollary is sampling bias: a single needle biopsy samples only a subset of terminal branches and can miss clinically actionable mutations—or mistake a branch event for a ubiquitous target—across the rest of the tumor. [Mel Greaves](mel-greaves.md) and colleagues documented an analogous branching ("variegated") architecture in childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Anderson et al., *Nature*, 2011), proving single-cell subclonal diversity exists even before overt relapse.

## Clinical consequences

- **Heterogeneity and targeted drug failure.** Inhibiting a branch mutation prunes only the subclones sitting on that limb, leaving trunk-only or parallel-branch lineages intact to repopulate the tumor.
- **Therapy as selection.** Maximum-tolerated-dose regimens select sharply for resistant branches while clearing drug-sensitive competitors that kept them suppressed — the competitive release dynamic managed by [adaptive therapy](adaptive-therapy.md).
- **Relapse from ancestral or minor clones.** Recurrent disease is frequently seeded not by the dominant pre-treatment bulk clone, but by a minor pre-existing branch or ancestral stem clone surviving the therapeutic bottleneck.

Clonal evolution is the foundation of [cancer ecology](cancer-ecology.md) and every evolutionary oncology strategy that treats malignancy as an adapting population rather than a fixed static genotype.
