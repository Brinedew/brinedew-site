# Tomasetti–Vogelstein model

The Tomasetti–Vogelstein model explains differences in cancer risk between tissues by the lifetime number of stem-cell divisions each tissue undergoes: the more replications a tissue's stem cells perform, the more opportunities random replication errors have to produce a driver mutation.

## The correlation

Tomasetti and Vogelstein plotted lifetime cancer incidence for 31 tissue types against the estimated cumulative number of stem-cell divisions in each, and found that the two correlate strongly (r = 0.81 in the 2015 "bad luck" paper). Cancers of the colon, with its constantly renewed epithelium, sit at the high-division end; bone and brain cancers sit at the low end. The 2017 follow-up classified cancer types by the fraction of driver mutations attributable to three sources: hereditary mutations, environmental exposures, and replication errors. Replication errors dominated in tissues with high division counts, and the authors estimated that about two-thirds of driver mutations across cancers arise this way.

## What the model claims, and what it does not

The correlation is a claim about the supply of mutations, and it explains why tissues differ in baseline risk. It is compatible with the large environmental fraction of cancer: smoking, UV, and HPV raise mutation rates in the tissues they reach, and the model's own 2017 analysis assigns those exposures their share. The public reading of the 2015 paper — that most cancer is "bad luck" and prevention is futile — was a misreading the authors explicitly corrected: prevention still works on the environmental share, and the replication-error share is exactly the component that [early detection](peter-nowell.md) and fitness-landscape approaches address.

The model matters for this wiki because it quantifies the [mutation supply](replicative-credit.md) side of somatic evolution: division count is the denominator that determines how often a tissue samples a driver, before selection decides what a driver is worth.
