# Leo Buss

![Leo Buss](../Attachments/leo-buss.jpg)

Leo W. Buss (born 1952) is an American evolutionary and developmental biologist whose work reframed multicellular organisms as negotiated evolutionary collectives rather than taken-for-granted units.

## Biography and career

Buss earned his B.A., M.A., and Ph.D. from Johns Hopkins University (1975, 1977, 1979) and joined the Yale University faculty in 1979, holding long-time appointments in ecology and evolutionary biology as well as geology and geophysics. At Yale he also served as a curator at the Peabody Museum of Natural History and as Founding Director of the Yale Institute for Biospheric Studies. In **1989**, he was named a **MacArthur Fellow**.

His empirical laboratory program focused heavily on colonial marine invertebrates, particularly the hydrozoan ***Hydractinia***, using colony fusion/rejection assays to investigate **allorecognition**, histocompatibility genetics, and somatic cell parasitism—cases where stem cells from one genetically distinct colony invade a fused partner and attempt to colonize its germ line.

## *The Evolution of Individuality* and its reception

Buss's 1987 book ***The Evolution of Individuality*** argued that classic neo-Darwinism treated multicellular individuals as static vehicles while ignoring conflict *inside* the body. Because cell lineages undergoing development can acquire heritable somatic variation, intra-organismal cell competition constantly threatens multicellular coherence. Buss proposed that conserved developmental rules—early sequestration of the germ line ([Weismann's barrier](weismann-barrier.md)), rigid early cleavage geometry, and limits on somatic cell lineage autonomy—evolved specifically as **conflict-mediation mechanisms** protecting organismal fitness against parasitic somatic variants.

The book initially drew vigorous debate from strict Weismannian population geneticists, who questioned how much somatic conflict actually shapes embryonic cleavage patterns outside basal metazoans. Over subsequent decades, however, *The Evolution of Individuality* gained broad recognition as a foundational text connecting evolutionary developmental biology, [evolutionary transitions in individuality](evolutionary-transitions-in-individuality.md), [de-Darwinization](de-darwinization.md), and evolutionary oncology (framing cancer as re-emergent lower-level selection). His later collaborative theoretical work with computer scientist **Walter Fontana** (at Yale and the Santa Fe Institute) extended organizational-closure and algorithmic-chemistry approaches to how self-maintaining biological organizations emerge from lower-level interactions.
