# Leo Buss

![Leo Buss](../Attachments/leo-buss.jpg)

**Leo W. Buss** (born 1952) is an American evolutionary and developmental biologist whose work reframed multicellular organisms as evolved collectives stabilized against internal cellular competition.[^1]

## Biography and empirical research

Buss earned his B.A., M.A., and Ph.D. from Johns Hopkins University (1975, 1977, 1979) and joined Yale University in 1979, holding appointments in ecology and evolutionary biology as well as geology and geophysics. At Yale he served as a curator at the Peabody Museum of Natural History and Founding Director of the Yale Institute for Biospheric Studies; in 1989 he was named a MacArthur Fellow.

His empirical work focused on colonial marine invertebrates, particularly the hydrozoan *Hydractinia*, using colony fusion assays to examine allorecognition, histocompatibility genetics, and somatic-cell parasitism in which stem cells from one colony invade a fused partner and attempt to enter its germline.

## *The Evolution of Individuality*

In *The Evolution of Individuality* (1987), Buss argued that neo-Darwinian theory treated organisms as unproblematic units while neglecting selection among dividing cell lineages inside a developing body.[^1] Because somatic mutations create heritable variation within an organism, cell lineages compete unless developmental rules suppress that competition. Buss proposed that early sequestration of the germline ([Weismann's barrier](weismann-barrier.md)), conserved cleavage patterns, and restrictions on somatic cell autonomy evolved as conflict-mediation mechanisms aligning cell behavior with organismal fitness.[^1]

The argument initially drew debate from population geneticists skeptical that somatic selection shapes embryonic morphogenesis outside basal metazoans. Over subsequent decades, however, *The Evolution of Individuality* became a foundational text bridging evolutionary developmental biology, [evolutionary transitions in individuality](evolutionary-transitions-in-individuality.md), [de-Darwinization](de-darwinization.md), and evolutionary oncology. Later theoretical work with computer scientist Walter Fontana examined how self-maintaining biological organizations arise from interactions among lower-level components.
