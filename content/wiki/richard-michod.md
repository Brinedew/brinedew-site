# Richard Michod

![Richard Michod](../Attachments/richard-michod.jpg)

Richard E. ("Rick") Michod is an American evolutionary biologist and professor emeritus at the University of Arizona, known for formalizing [evolutionary transitions in individuality](evolutionary-transitions-in-individuality.md) (ETIs) and using volvocine green algae to demonstrate empirically how reproductive division of labor emerges from cellular trade-offs.

## Biography

Michod earned a B.S. in zoology from Duke University (1973), followed by an M.A. in mathematics (1978) and a Ph.D. in genetics and zoology (1978) from the University of Georgia. He joined the University of Arizona faculty in 1978, rising to Full Professor in 1987 and serving as Head of the Department of Ecology and Evolutionary Biology.

## Evolutionary transitions and fitness reorganization

Across *Darwinian Dynamics: Evolutionary Transitions in Fitness and Individuality* (1999) and companion theoretical papers, Michod treated the emergence of protocells, eukaryotic genomes, sex, and multicellular bodies as instances of a single mathematical transformation. An ETI progresses through two intertwined shifts:

1. **Conflict mediation.** Suppressing within-group selection among constituent cells (through mechanisms such as single-cell bottlenecks, early germline isolation, and policing) so cheating variants cannot overrun the emerging collective.
2. **Fitness export and reorganization.** Transferring fitness from the two original components—viability and reproduction—out of autonomous cells and into a collective division of labor, where soma handles survival and motility while dedicated germ cells handle heredity.

## The volvocine algae program

To bridge mathematical theory with living intermediate stages, Michod's group (alongside collaborators including Aurora Nedelcu) turned to the **volvocine green algae**—a monophyletic clade spanning unicellular *Chlamydomonas*, simple undifferentiated colonial forms, and multicellular *Volvox carteri* (~2,000 sterile somatic cells enclosing specialized germ cells, `gonidia`).

Their empirical and comparative work showed that reproductive altruism is anchored in flagellation constraints (single algae cells struggle to swim efficiently while dividing) and identified the molecular switch locking soma out of reproduction: the master regulator gene ***regA*** in *Volvox carteri*. *regA* suppresses somatic cell division by downregulating chloroplast biogenesis; strikingly, Michod and Nedelcu demonstrated that *regA* evolved by co-opting an ancestral unicellular stress-response trade-off (where ancestors paused division to survive adverse environments). When *regA* malfunctions, somatic cells revert to autonomous proliferation, offering a living evolutionary parallel to cellular cheating in somatic tumors.
