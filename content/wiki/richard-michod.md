# Richard Michod

![Richard Michod](../Attachments/richard-michod.jpg)

**Richard E. ("Rick") Michod** is an American evolutionary biologist and professor emeritus at the University of Arizona known for formalizing [evolutionary transitions in individuality](evolutionary-transitions-in-individuality.md) (ETIs) and using volvocine green algae to demonstrate how reproductive division of labor emerges from cellular trade-offs.[^1][^2]

## Biography

Michod earned a B.S. in zoology from Duke University (1973), followed by an M.A. in mathematics (1978) and a Ph.D. in genetics and zoology (1978) from the University of Georgia. He joined the University of Arizona faculty in 1978, becoming Full Professor in 1987 and serving as Head of the Department of Ecology and Evolutionary Biology.

## Evolutionary transitions and fitness reorganization

In *Darwinian Dynamics: Evolutionary Transitions in Fitness and Individuality* (1999) and companion theoretical work, Michod modeled the emergence of protocells, eukaryotic genomes, sex, and multicellular bodies as instances of a general mathematical transformation.[^1] An evolutionary transition progresses through two coupled processes:

1. **Conflict mediation.** Suppressing within-group selection among lower-level units (via single-cell bottlenecks, early germline isolation, and policing) so cheating variants cannot overrun the collective.
2. **Fitness export and reorganization.** Transferring fitness components—viability and reproduction—from autonomous cells to a collective division of labor, in which soma handles survival and motility while dedicated germ cells handle heredity.

## The volvocine algae program

To link mathematical theory to living intermediate stages, Michod and collaborators including Aurora Nedelcu studied the volvocine green algae—a clade spanning unicellular *Chlamydomonas*, simple undifferentiated colonies, and multicellular *Volvox carteri* (~2,000 sterile somatic cells enclosing specialized germ cells, `gonidia`).[^2]

Their comparative and molecular work showed that reproductive altruism is anchored in a motility–mitosis trade-off—single algal cells cannot easily swim with flagella while dividing—and traced somatic sterilization in *Volvox carteri* to the master regulator gene *regA*, which suppresses somatic division by downregulating chloroplast biogenesis.[^2] They further demonstrated that *regA* evolved by co-opting an ancestral unicellular stress-response pathway that transiently halted division under adverse conditions; when *regA* malfunctions, somatic cells revert to autonomous proliferation, offering an evolutionary parallel to somatic cheating in tumors.
