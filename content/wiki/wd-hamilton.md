# W. D. Hamilton

![W. D. Hamilton](../Attachments/wd-hamilton.jpg)

**William Donald Hamilton** (1936–2000) was a British evolutionary biologist who formulated inclusive fitness theory (kin selection), formalized the evolutionary [force of selection](selection-shadow-theory.md) underlying senescence, pioneered sex-ratio game theory, and developed the parasite-driven [Red Queen hypothesis](red-queen-hypothesis.md) for the evolution of sex.[^1][^2][^3][^4]

## Inclusive fitness and *Narrow Roads of Gene Land*

After academic posts including Imperial College London and a professorship at the University of Oxford, Hamilton collected his major papers in the three-volume ***Narrow Roads of Gene Land***, accompanied by autobiographical introductions to each piece:[^5]

- **Volume 1:** *Evolution of Social Behaviour* (1996) — anchored by his 1964 formulation of inclusive fitness and Hamilton's rule ($rb > c$) as the genetic foundation for social behavior and altruism.[^1]
- **Volume 2:** *Evolution of Sex* (2001) — centered on sexual reproduction, sex ratios, and host–parasite coevolution.
- **Volume 3:** *Last Words* (2005, posthumous) — late theoretical essays and field reflections.

## Extraordinary sex ratios

In 1967, Hamilton explained why R. A. Fisher's classical argument for an equal 1:1 sex ratio fails whenever mating occurs in subdivided patches rather than a panmictic population.[^3] When brothers compete locally with one another for mates (**local mate competition**, as in parasitic wasps and fig wasps), selection favors mothers producing heavily female-biased broods—enough sons only to inseminate the sisters in the patch.[^3] Hamilton also analyzed driving X-linked and Y-linked sex-ratio distorters as intragenomic conflicts, introducing unbeatable-strategy reasoning that directly anticipated [John Maynard Smith](john-maynard-smith.md)'s evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS).

## Force of selection, parasites, and senescence

Hamilton derived quantitative indicator functions in 1966 showing that the [force of selection](selection-shadow-theory.md) on age-specific survival and fertility inevitably declines with age after reproductive onset, supplying the mathematical basis for Peter Medawar's mutation-accumulation model and [George C. Williams](george-c-williams.md)'s [antagonistic pleiotropy](antagonistic-pleiotropy-theory.md).[^2] He later argued that cycling host–parasite coevolution maintains sexual recombination despite its twofold cost to females.[^4]

Hamilton received the Crafoord Prize and the Kyoto Prize, and died in 2000 from complications of malaria contracted during fieldwork in the Congo.
