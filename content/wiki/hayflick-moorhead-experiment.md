# Hayflick–Moorhead experiment

The **Hayflick–Moorhead experiment** was a 1961 study of normal human fibroblasts in culture that established a finite replicative lifespan for these cells. Leonard Hayflick and Paul Moorhead grew human fetal lung fibroblasts through serial passages and counted the divisions until the cultures stopped expanding, at roughly fifty population doublings. The arrested cells stayed alive and metabolically active, a state now called [[cellular-senescence|cellular senescence]]. The result replaced the prevailing view that normal animal cells divide indefinitely, and it turned the division count into a measurable phenotype that aging research could study.[^1][^3]

## Background

Early tissue-culture work produced cell lines that appeared to grow indefinitely, and the established view held that the culture medium set the only ceiling on division while aging acted on cells from the outside.[^3] By the 1950s, methods based on chick-embryo extract and frequent subculturing kept many lines proliferating, which made a division limit belonging to the cell itself hard to observe. Hayflick, then preparing human fetal cells for vaccine work at the Wistar Institute, noticed that his cultures ran down after a characteristic number of passages and set out to test whether the ceiling came from the cells or from the technique.[^3]

## Design

Hayflick and Moorhead established diploid fibroblast strains from human fetal tissue and passed them serially, diluting and replating the cells at fixed intervals. They recorded population doublings as the primary measure, followed several strains in parallel, and preserved samples for chromosome counting. The design separated two candidate sources of the limit: the culture conditions and the cells themselves. A co-culture that combined cells with different passage histories, distinguished by chromosome markers, showed that each population stopped dividing at its own division count, which located the limit inside the cell.[^1][^3]

## Result

Cultures completed 40 to 60 population doublings and then halted. The arrested cells stayed attached to the dish, enlarged, and survived for months while continuing their metabolic activity. Replenishing medium and serum failed to restart division in either the original flasks or their subcultures, which located the cause in the cells. Chromosome counts confirmed that the populations stayed diploid, so the arrest reflected ordinary cell physiology. Hayflick and Moorhead called the surviving, division-arrested population "senescent."

## What it established

The experiment defined replicative senescence and produced three lasting results:

- Normal human fibroblasts have a finite division capacity that is reproducible across strains.
- Division arrest is compatible with continued survival and metabolic activity, so senescence is a state distinct from cell death.
- The limit belongs to the individual cell lineage, which made the division count a candidate clock for cellular aging.

The finding connected culture biology to organismal aging. Fibroblast division capacity varies with donor age and with species lifespan, and the limit later mapped onto the shortening of [[telomeres]] with each round of replication.[^3] The finite lifespan also explains why normal cells are mortal while cancer cells achieve indefinite proliferation, a contrast central to [[oncogene-classification|oncogene classification]] and to work on [[telomerase-tert|telomerase]].[^3]

## Limits and later work

The 1961 study measured a culture phenotype, and it left open how replicative arrest contributes to aging in a whole organism. A 1965 follow-up extended the observation across additional strains and tied the limit to chromosome changes.[^2] The [[serrano-lowe-senescence-experiment|Serrano–Lowe senescence experiment]] later showed that oncogenic signaling triggers the same arrest early, which recast senescence as a stress response and a barrier to tumor formation. The [[kenyon-daf-2-experiment|Kenyon daf-2 experiment]] and the [[baker-ink-attac-experiment|Baker INK-ATTAC experiment]] carried the study of aging into regulated longevity pathways and the clearance of senescent cells in live animals.
