# Carlo Maley

![Carlo Maley](../Attachments/carlo-maley.jpg)

Carlo C. Maley is an American evolutionary biologist and computer scientist, professor at Arizona State University and director of the Arizona Cancer Evolution Center. His work applies evolutionary and [ecological theory](cancer-ecology.md) to neoplastic progression, risk prediction, and comparative oncology.

## Career

Maley received his PhD in computer science from MIT and held faculty and research positions at the Wistar Institute and the University of California, San Francisco (where he co-founded the Center for Evolution and Cancer with [Athena Aktipis](athena-aktipis.md)) before joining Arizona State University in 2015 at the Biodesign Institute and School of Life Sciences.

## Contributions

- **Evolutionary ecology synthesis (*Nature Reviews Cancer*, 2006).** With Lauren Merlo, John Pepper, and Brian Reid, Maley authored *"Cancer as an evolutionary and ecological process,"* which synthesized somatic mutation, clonal selection, microenvironmental hazards, and therapeutic resistance within a formal population-ecology framework. A follow-up framework (*Nature Reviews Cancer*, 2017) introduced an Evo-Eco classification system indexing tumors by both evolutionary dynamics (diversity, mutational turnover) and ecological habitat traits.
- **Clonal diversity and neoplastic progression in Barrett's esophagus.** Studying patients with Barrett's esophagus — a premalignant metaplastic state with measurable progression risk — Maley et al. (*Nature Genetics*, 2006) showed that ecological measures of genetic clonal diversity within biopsies strongly predict progression to esophageal adenocarcinoma. Later single-crypt and multi-biopsy work (*Nature Communications*, 2017) mapped the spatial–temporal expansion of mutant clones across crypts prior to genome doubling and overt malignancy.
- **Cancer across the tree of life.** As director of the Arizona Cancer Evolution Center, Maley coordinates large comparative surveys of neoplasm and malignancy rates across vertebrates, confronting [Peto's paradox](petos-paradox.md) by measuring how body size, lifespan, and life-history strategy shape evolved tumor-suppression mechanisms across species.
