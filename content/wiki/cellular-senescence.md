# Cellular senescence

**Cellular senescence** is a state in which a cell stops dividing, usually irreversibly, but stays alive and metabolically active. Senescent cells resist the signals that would normally make a damaged cell kill itself, and many of them secrete a mixture of inflammatory proteins, growth factors and enzymes that remodel tissue.[^campisi2007][^gorgoulis2019] Leonard Hayflick and Paul Moorhead first observed the arrest in 1961, in human cells that stopped dividing after a limited number of divisions in culture.[^hayflick1961] Cells are now known to enter senescence when their telomeres become too short, when their DNA is damaged, when oncogenes are activated, and during normal embryonic development.[^dadda2003][^serrano1997][^munozespin2013]

Senescence stops cells that are at risk of becoming cancerous from dividing, and senescent cells are abundant in premalignant lesions in mice and people.[^collado2005][^michaloglou2005] In mice, senescent cells also help skin wounds heal and limit liver fibrosis.[^demaria2014][^krizhanovsky2008] They become more common in many tissues with age,[^tuttle2020] and in one widely cited mouse study, killing cells with high p16 expression from middle age extended median lifespan by 24–27%, though not maximum lifespan in the inbred strain.[^baker2016]

Three questions run through the field. The first is how much senescent cells contribute to ageing. In mice, removing cells with high p16 or p21 delays several age-related changes, but the lifespan gains are modest, have not been reproduced by an independent laboratory, and did not extend maximum lifespan in inbred mice; in human tissues the rise of senescence markers with age is weak and varies between tissues and markers.[^baker2016][^tuttle2020] Senescent cells contribute to some age-related pathology in mice; that they are a main driver of mammalian ageing has not been shown. The second is which cells marked as senescent cause harm. That question is settled in favour of heterogeneity: cells with high p16 include endothelial cells whose removal causes fibrosis, a reparative niche in the lung and macrophages that are not senescent at all, so "p16-positive" does not mean "senescent and harmful".[^grosse2020][^reyes2022][^hall2016] The third is whether drugs that kill senescent cells, [[senolytics|*senolytics*]], benefit people. No randomised trial has yet shown a clinical benefit: a phase 2 trial of the senolytic UBX0101 in knee osteoarthritis missed its primary endpoint, and randomised pilot trials of dasatinib plus quercetin in pulmonary fibrosis and in postmenopausal bone loss found no difference from control on their main outcomes.[^unity2020][^nambiar2023][^farr2024]

## Terminology

- **Replicative senescence** is the arrest that ends the division of human cells in culture after a limited number of divisions, the *Hayflick limit*. It is triggered by short telomeres (see [[telomeres]] and [[hayflick-moorhead-experiment]]).
- **Oncogene-induced senescence** is senescence triggered by an activated oncogene, such as mutant *RAS* or *BRAF*, independently of the number of divisions.[^serrano1997][^michaloglou2005]
- **Quiescence** is a reversible pause in division, from which a cell returns when conditions change. Senescence differs in being stable, and in the cell's changed shape, metabolism and secretions.[^campisi2007]
- The **senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)** is the set of proteins senescent cells secrete, including inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-8.[^coppe2008] (See [[sasp]].)
- **Senolytics** are drugs that kill senescent cells selectively; **senomorphics** suppress the SASP without killing the cells. (See [[senolytics]].)
- **Markers.** No single marker identifies senescent cells. The most used are the activity of a β-galactosidase enzyme detectable at pH 6 (*SA-β-gal*), high levels of the cell-cycle inhibitors p16 and p21, and the absence of markers of division; a 2019 consensus recommended combining several.[^dimri1995][^gorgoulis2019]

## Discovery

Hayflick and Moorhead described 25 strains of normal human fibroblasts derived from fetuses and listed their finite lifetime in culture as one of the criteria that distinguished them from cancer cell lines.[^hayflick1961] In 1965 Hayflick reported that the time at which such strains stop dividing "is not a function of the number of subcultivations but rather of the number of potential cell doublings", which he put at 50±10. Cells from adult donors stopped after about 20 doublings.[^hayflick1965]

The counting mechanism was found three decades later. Telomeres shorten as human cells divide in culture,[^harley1990] and cells given telomerase, the enzyme that lengthens telomeres, continued dividing at least 20 doublings past their normal limit.[^bodnar1998] Fabrizio d'Adda di Fagagna and colleagues showed in 2003 that the arrest is a DNA-damage response: senescent human cells carry clusters of DNA-damage proteins at their chromosome ends, and inactivating the checkpoint kinases CHK1 and CHK2 lets them re-enter DNA replication.[^dadda2003]

In 1997 Manuel Serrano, Scott Lowe and colleagues found that an activated *RAS* oncogene causes normal human and rodent cells to enter "a permanent G1 arrest" that is "phenotypically indistinguishable from cellular senescence". Senescence, they concluded, "does not simply reflect the accumulation of cell divisions, but can be prematurely activated in response to an oncogenic stimulus."[^serrano1997]

## Pathways and markers

Two tumour-suppressor pathways establish and maintain the arrest (see [[p53-tp53|p53]], [[p21cip1-cdkn1a|p21]], [[p16ink4a-cdkn2a|p16]] and [[rb-rb1|RB]]).

- **p53 and p21.** DNA damage, including damage at short telomeres, activates p53, which induces p21. p21 inhibits the cyclin-dependent kinases that drive the cell into DNA replication.
- **p16 and RB.** p16 inhibits the kinases CDK4 and CDK6, which leaves the RB protein active. Active RB represses the [[e2f1-e2f1-q01094|E2F]] transcription factors that cells need to replicate DNA.

The two pathways differ in how they hold the arrest. Christian Beauséjour and colleagues inactivated p53 in senescent human fibroblasts: cells with low p16 resumed growth, but cells with high p16 did not. Telomerase alone did not reverse the arrest. The arrest that follows telomere dysfunction is therefore maintained mainly by p53, and p16 provides a second barrier that, once established, makes it hard to reverse.[^beausejour2003]

Senescent cells were first identified in tissue by Goberdhan Dimri and colleagues, who found that "several human cells express a beta-galactosidase, histochemically detectable at pH 6, upon senescence in culture", and that cells staining for it become more common in human skin with age.[^dimri1995] The stained enzyme is ordinary lysosomal β-galactosidase, whose activity at pH 6 reflects lysosomal mass,[^lee2006] and macrophages that express both it and p16 accumulate in old mice.[^hall2016] Later work showed that senescent cells are heterogeneous. Comparing many transcriptomes, Alejandra Hernandez-Segura and colleagues found that the senescence programme depends on cell type, on the stress that caused it and on time since arrest, and identified 55 genes at its common core, none of them a classical marker such as p16 or p21.[^hernandezsegura2017] The International Cell Senescence Association therefore recommended identifying senescent cells by several markers together.[^gorgoulis2019]

## The secretory phenotype

Jean-Philippe Coppé, Judith Campisi and colleagues named the SASP in 2008. Human cells induced to senesce by DNA damage "secrete myriad factors associated with inflammation and malignancy"; the secretion developed over several days, only after damage severe enough to cause senescence, and was amplified by oncogenic RAS and by loss of p53.[^coppe2008] The same year, two groups showed that the SASP also acts on the senescent cell itself. Juan Carlos Acosta and colleagues found that chemokines acting through the receptor CXCR2 reinforce senescence,[^acosta2008] and Thomas Kuilman and colleagues found that IL-6 is required for oncogene-induced senescence to begin and to persist.[^kuilman2008]

The SASP makes senescence a property of tissues as well as cells. It recruits immune cells that clear senescent cells, and it can also promote inflammation and, in some contexts, the growth of neighbouring premalignant cells.[^coppe2008][^campisi2005] (See [[sasp]].)

## Functions

### Tumour suppression

In 2005 four groups found senescence in early tumours. Manuel Collado and colleagues found senescence markers abundant in premalignant lung adenomas driven by *K-ras* in mice, and essentially absent from the adenocarcinomas that develop from them.[^collado2005] Chrysiis Michaloglou and colleagues found that sustained mutant *BRAF* arrests human melanocytes, and that congenital naevi (moles), most of which carry this mutation, are "invariably positive" for SA-β-gal without telomere attrition.[^michaloglou2005] Later studies found that the usual senescence markers do not distinguish naevus cells from normal or malignant melanocytes, and in a mouse model naevus cells showed no evidence of senescence, so whether naevi are held by senescence is disputed.[^tran2012][^ruizvega2020] Zhenbang Chen and colleagues found that loss of *Pten* in the mouse prostate induces p53-dependent senescence, so that invasive cancer develops early only when p53 is also lost.[^chen2005] Melanie Braig and colleagues found that oncogene-induced senescence is a barrier to lymphoma in mice.[^braig2005]

The immune system removes senescent premalignant and tumour cells. Wen Xue and colleagues found that restoring p53 in mouse liver carcinomas caused complete regressions, mainly by senescence followed by "an innate immune response that targeted the tumour cells".[^xue2007] Tae-Won Kang and colleagues found that hepatocytes made senescent by oncogenic *Nras* are cleared by an immune response that requires CD4+ T cells, which limits the development of liver cancer.[^kang2011]

### Repair and development

Senescent cells appear early in skin wounds and speed their closure by secreting the growth factor PDGF-AA; clearing them delays healing.[^demaria2014] In damaged mouse liver, activated stellate cells become senescent, make less scar tissue and are killed by natural killer cells, which limits fibrosis; mice that cannot activate senescence develop more fibrosis.[^krizhanovsky2008]

Senescence also occurs in normal embryos. Daniel Muñoz-Espín and colleagues found it in the mesonephros (a transient embryonic kidney) and the endolymphatic sac of the inner ear of mouse and human embryos, dependent on p21 but independent of DNA damage and p53, and followed by clearance by macrophages; they proposed that this programme "is the evolutionary origin of damage-induced senescence".[^munozespin2013] Mekayla Storer and colleagues found senescent cells in the signalling centre that directs limb growth, where loss of p21 caused defects in limb patterning.[^storer2013]

## Senescent cells and ageing

### Accumulation

In tissue arrays from five donors per age group, cells staining for p16 or p21 increased with age in skin, kidney and pancreas, did not increase in lung, and were undetectable in skeletal and cardiac muscle.[^idda2020] A meta-analysis of 38 human studies found a modest overall correlation between senescence markers and age (*r* = 0.28; 95% prediction interval −0.12 to 0.60). It was strongest for DNA-damage markers (*r* = 0.69) and absent for proliferation markers (*r* = 0.08), and the level of senescence "varies considerably depending upon tissue type, tissue section, and marker used to detect senescence".[^tuttle2020]

How long senescent cells persist depends on how fast they are removed. Omer Karin, Uri Alon and colleagues reported that senescent cells "turn over rapidly in young mice, with a half-life of days, but slow their own removal rate to a half-life of weeks in old mice". Their model, fitted to whole-body p16-reporter signals from 33 mice, predicted half-lives of about 5 days at 3 months and 25 days at 22 months. After inducing senescence in lung epithelium, they measured about 4.7 days in young mice and about 18 days, with wide uncertainty and only two sampling days, in 22-month-old mice.[^karin2019] Mice with impaired cytotoxic immune cells, lacking perforin, accumulate more senescent cells and have shorter lives.[^ovadya2018]

### A model of senescent-cell dynamics

*Main article: [[saturating-removal-model-of-senescence|Saturating-removal model of senescence]]*

Karin and colleagues proposed that senescent cells are produced at a rate that rises in proportion to age and removed at a rate that saturates when they are numerous, and assumed, for illustration, that death occurs when their burden first crosses a threshold. The model fitted whole-body p16-reporter trajectories from 33 mice better than variants without saturating removal, and, with a separate set of parameters chosen to match the lifespans of C57BL/6J mice, gives a mortality rate that rises roughly exponentially with age, as in the [[Mortality rate models#gompertz-law|Gompertz law]].[^karin2019] Neither fit singles it out. A model with constant production and a fraction of senescent cells that cannot be removed fits the same reporter data comparably;[^kowald2020] lineage tracing found half-lives of 2.6 to 4.2 months for naturally arising cells with high p16 expression;[^omori2020] and the same equation, with the burden read as generic damage, fits the survival curves of species from bacteria to humans.[^raz2026]

### Clearing senescent cells in mice

Darren Baker, Jan van Deursen and colleagues built mice in which cells expressing p16 can be killed with a drug (see [[baker-ink-attac-experiment]]). In mice with a progeroid mutation, life-long removal of these cells delayed age-related disease in fat, muscle and eye, and removal late in life "attenuated progression of already established age-related disorders".[^baker2011] In normal mice, removal twice a week from 12 months of age extended median lifespan by 27% in one genetic background and 24% in another, delayed tumour formation and attenuated the deterioration of kidney, heart and fat.[^baker2016] Clearance was partial and did not reach liver or colon; maximum lifespan was not extended in C57BL/6 mice; and the authors noted that vehicle-injected male C57BL/6 controls were short-lived, "suggesting that repetitive vehicle injection stress may have negatively impacted C57BL/6 male longevity".[^baker2016] Removing cells that express high levels of p21, once a month from 20 months of age, extended both median and maximum lifespan.[^wang2024]

Transplanting small numbers of senescent cells into young mice caused persistent physical dysfunction and spread senescence to the host's tissues.[^xu2018] Drugs that remove senescent cells are described below and in [[senolytics|Senolytics]].

Not all cells with high p16 are harmful to remove. In p16 reporter mice, most such cells that appear from about 10–12 months were vascular endothelial cells, mostly in the liver sinusoids, with fewer macrophages and adipocytes. Removing them, continuously or acutely, disrupted blood–tissue barriers, caused liver and perivascular fibrosis and worsened health, and the endothelial cells were not replaced.[^grosse2020] In the lung, p16-expressing fibroblasts in the basement membrane form a niche that supports repair of the epithelium.[^reyes2022] Clearing senescent cells from skin wounds delays healing.[^demaria2014]

## Senolytic drugs

*Main article: [[senolytics|Senolytics]]*

Senolytics are drugs that kill senescent cells selectively, by blocking the anti-apoptotic pathways on which senescent cells depend. The first, the combination of the cancer drug dasatinib and the flavonoid quercetin, was reported in 2015; inhibitors of the BCL-2 family of survival proteins, such as navitoclax, followed.[^zhu2015][^chang2016] In mice they reduce markers of senescence and improve several age-related measures, but the lifespan results are few and unreplicated. Dasatinib plus quercetin started at 24–27 months lengthened median survival from the start of treatment by 36% in one study.[^xu2018] Fisetin, reported to extend lifespan in groups of eight or nine mice,[^yousefzadeh2018] had no effect on lifespan in the Interventions Testing Program.[^harrison2024]

In people, no randomised trial has shown a clinical benefit. A phase 2 trial of UBX0101 in knee osteoarthritis missed its primary endpoint,[^unity2020] and randomised pilot trials of dasatinib plus quercetin in pulmonary fibrosis and in postmenopausal bone loss found no difference from control on their main outcomes.[^nambiar2023][^farr2024] The uncontrolled early studies that reported falls in senescence markers were small, and one was later corrected.[^hickson2019][^hickson2020] Much of this work comes from the group that developed dasatinib plus quercetin, whose members hold patents on senolytic drugs.[^chaib2022]

## Senescence in theories of ageing

Campisi proposed in 2005 that senescence is antagonistically pleiotropic: it promotes survival early in life by suppressing cancer, and limits it later as senescent cells accumulate.[^campisi2005] Silvia Giaimo and d'Adda di Fagagna questioned whether the evidence shows that the benefits fall mainly in youth and the costs mainly in old age.[^giaimo2012] Senescence also continues to suppress cancer in old organisms, which bears on whether removing senescent cells late in life is safe. (See [[antagonistic-pleiotropy-theory#cellular-senescence|Antagonistic pleiotropy]] and [[tumor-suppressor-theory-of-aging]].)

## History

### Replicative senescence

Hayflick and Moorhead established in 1961 that normal human cells have a finite lifetime in culture, and Hayflick related it to the number of divisions in 1965.[^hayflick1961][^hayflick1965] In 1995 Dimri and colleagues found a marker that identified senescent cells in tissue,[^dimri1995] and between 1990 and 2003 shortening telomeres and the DNA-damage response at chromosome ends were identified as the mechanism of the replicative limit.[^harley1990][^bodnar1998][^dadda2003]

### Senescence beyond the Hayflick limit

Oncogene-induced senescence was described in 1997,[^serrano1997] and in 2005 four studies found senescence in premalignant tumours.[^collado2005][^michaloglou2005][^chen2005][^braig2005] The SASP was named in 2008,[^coppe2008] and senescence in normal embryonic development was described in 2013.[^munozespin2013][^storer2013]

### Senescent cells as a target

In 2011 and 2016 Baker and colleagues showed that removing p16-expressing cells delays age-related disease and extends lifespan in mice,[^baker2011][^baker2016] and the first senolytic drugs were reported in 2015.[^zhu2015] The first human pilot studies were published in 2019,[^justice2019][^hickson2019] and in 2020 the first placebo-controlled phase 2 trial of a senolytic failed.[^unity2020] Randomised trials of dasatinib plus quercetin published in 2023 and 2024 were also negative on their primary or main outcomes.[^nambiar2023][^farr2024]
