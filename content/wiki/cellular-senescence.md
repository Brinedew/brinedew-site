# Cellular senescence

**Cellular senescence** is a state in which a cell permanently stops dividing but stays alive and metabolically active. Senescent cells resist the signals that would normally make a damaged cell kill itself, and many of them secrete a mixture of inflammatory proteins, growth factors and enzymes that remodel tissue.[^campisi2007][^gorgoulis2019] Leonard Hayflick and Paul Moorhead first observed the arrest in 1961, in human cells that stopped dividing after a limited number of divisions in culture.[^hayflick1961] Cells are now known to enter senescence when their telomeres become too short, when their DNA is damaged, when oncogenes are activated, and during normal embryonic development.[^dadda2003][^serrano1997][^munozespin2013]

Senescence stops cells that are at risk of becoming cancerous from dividing, and senescent cells are abundant in premalignant lesions in mice and people.[^collado2005][^michaloglou2005] Senescent cells also help wounds heal and limit fibrosis.[^demaria2014][^krizhanovsky2008] They become more common in many tissues with age,[^tuttle2020] and in mice, killing them extends median lifespan by about a quarter.[^baker2016]

Three questions run through the field. The first is how much senescent cells contribute to human ageing; the evidence is strongest in mice, and in human tissues the rise of senescence markers with age is modest and varies between tissues and markers.[^tuttle2020] The second is which senescent cells cause harm and which do necessary work; removing some kinds of senescent cells in mice causes fibrosis.[^grosse2020] The third is whether drugs that kill senescent cells, *senolytics*, benefit people. Early human studies have been small, and the one completed placebo-controlled trial failed.[^unity2020]

## Terminology

- **Replicative senescence** is the arrest that ends the division of human cells in culture after a limited number of divisions, the *Hayflick limit*. It is triggered by short telomeres (see [[telomeres]] and [[hayflick-moorhead-experiment]]).
- **Oncogene-induced senescence** is senescence triggered by an activated oncogene, such as mutant *RAS* or *BRAF*, independently of the number of divisions.[^serrano1997][^michaloglou2005]
- **Quiescence** is a reversible pause in division, from which a cell returns when conditions change. Senescence differs in being stable, and in the cell's changed shape, metabolism and secretions.[^campisi2007]
- The **senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)** is the set of proteins senescent cells secrete, including inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-8.[^coppe2008] (See [[sasp]].)
- **Senolytics** are drugs that kill senescent cells selectively; **senomorphics** suppress the SASP without killing the cells. (See [[senolytics]].)
- **Markers.** No single marker identifies senescent cells. The most used are the activity of a β-galactosidase enzyme detectable at pH 6 (*SA-β-gal*), high levels of the cell-cycle inhibitors p16 and p21, and the absence of markers of division; a 2019 consensus recommended combining several.[^dimri1995][^gorgoulis2019]

## Discovery

Hayflick and Moorhead described 25 strains of normal human fibroblasts derived from fetuses and listed their finite lifetime in culture as one of the criteria that distinguished them from cancer cell lines.[^hayflick1961] In 1965 Hayflick reported that the time at which such strains stop dividing "is not a function of the number of subcultivations but rather of the number of potential cell doublings", which he put at 50±10. Cells from adult donors stopped after about 20 doublings.[^hayflick1965]

The counting mechanism was found three decades later. Telomeres shorten as human cells divide in culture,[^harley1990] and cells given telomerase, the enzyme that lengthens telomeres, divide far beyond their normal limit.[^bodnar1998] Fabrizio d'Adda di Fagagna and colleagues showed in 2003 that the arrest is a DNA-damage response: senescent human cells carry clusters of DNA-damage proteins at their chromosome ends, and inactivating the checkpoint kinases CHK1 and CHK2 lets them resume division.[^dadda2003]

In 1997 Manuel Serrano, Scott Lowe and colleagues found that an activated *RAS* oncogene causes normal human and rodent cells to enter "a permanent G1 arrest" that is "phenotypically indistinguishable from cellular senescence". Senescence, they concluded, "does not simply reflect the accumulation of cell divisions, but can be prematurely activated in response to an oncogenic stimulus."[^serrano1997]

## Pathways and markers

Two tumour-suppressor pathways establish and maintain the arrest (see [[p53-tp53|p53]], [[p21cip1-cdkn1a|p21]], [[p16ink4a-cdkn2a|p16]] and [[rb-rb1|RB]]).

- **p53 and p21.** DNA damage, including damage at short telomeres, activates p53, which induces p21. p21 inhibits the cyclin-dependent kinases that drive the cell into DNA replication.
- **p16 and RB.** p16 inhibits the kinases CDK4 and CDK6, which leaves the RB protein active. Active RB represses the [[e2f1-e2f1-q01094|E2F]] transcription factors that cells need to replicate DNA.

The two pathways differ in how they hold the arrest. Christian Beauséjour and colleagues inactivated p53 in senescent human fibroblasts: cells with low p16 resumed growth, but cells with high p16 did not. Telomerase alone did not reverse the arrest. The arrest that follows telomere dysfunction is therefore maintained mainly by p53, and p16 provides a second barrier that, once established, makes it hard to reverse.[^beausejour2003]

Senescent cells were first identified in tissue by Goberdhan Dimri and colleagues, who found that "several human cells express a beta-galactosidase, histochemically detectable at pH 6, upon senescence in culture", and that cells staining for it become more common in human skin with age.[^dimri1995] Later work showed that senescent cells are heterogeneous. Comparing many transcriptomes, Alejandra Hernandez-Segura and colleagues found that the senescence programme depends on cell type, on the stress that caused it and on time since arrest, and identified 55 genes at its common core.[^hernandezsegura2017] The International Cell Senescence Association therefore recommended identifying senescent cells by several markers together.[^gorgoulis2019]

## The secretory phenotype

Jean-Philippe Coppé, Judith Campisi and colleagues named the SASP in 2008. Human cells induced to senesce by DNA damage "secrete myriad factors associated with inflammation and malignancy"; the secretion developed over several days, only after damage severe enough to cause senescence, and was amplified by oncogenic RAS and by loss of p53.[^coppe2008] The same year, two groups showed that the SASP also acts on the senescent cell itself. Juan Carlos Acosta and colleagues found that chemokines acting through the receptor CXCR2 reinforce senescence,[^acosta2008] and Thomas Kuilman and colleagues found that IL-6 is required for oncogene-induced senescence to begin and to persist.[^kuilman2008]

The SASP makes senescence a property of tissues as well as cells. It recruits immune cells that clear senescent cells, and it can also promote inflammation and, in some contexts, the growth of neighbouring premalignant cells.[^coppe2008][^campisi2005] (See [[sasp]].)

## Functions

### Tumour suppression

In 2005 four groups found senescence in early tumours. Manuel Collado and colleagues found senescence markers abundant in premalignant lung adenomas driven by *K-ras* in mice, and essentially absent from the adenocarcinomas that develop from them.[^collado2005] Chrysiis Michaloglou and colleagues found that sustained mutant *BRAF* arrests human melanocytes, and that human naevi (moles), which carry this mutation, are "invariably positive" for SA-β-gal without showing telomere attrition.[^michaloglou2005] Zhenbang Chen and colleagues found that loss of *Pten* in the mouse prostate induces p53-dependent senescence, so that invasive cancer develops early only when p53 is also lost.[^chen2005] Melanie Braig and colleagues found that oncogene-induced senescence is a barrier to lymphoma in mice.[^braig2005]

The immune system removes senescent premalignant cells. Wen Xue and colleagues found that restoring p53 in mouse liver carcinomas caused complete regressions, mainly by senescence followed by "an innate immune response that targeted the tumour cells".[^xue2007] Tae-Won Kang and colleagues found that hepatocytes made senescent by oncogenic *Nras* are cleared by an immune response that requires CD4+ T cells, which limits the development of liver cancer.[^kang2011]

### Repair and development

Senescent cells appear early in skin wounds and speed their closure by secreting the growth factor PDGF-AA; clearing them delays healing.[^demaria2014] In damaged mouse liver, activated stellate cells become senescent, make less scar tissue and are killed by natural killer cells, which limits fibrosis; mice that cannot activate senescence develop more fibrosis.[^krizhanovsky2008]

Senescence also occurs in normal embryos. Daniel Muñoz-Espín and colleagues found it in the developing kidney and inner ear of mice and human embryos, dependent on p21 but independent of DNA damage and p53, and followed by clearance by macrophages; they proposed that this programme "is the evolutionary origin of damage-induced senescence".[^munozespin2013] Mekayla Storer and colleagues found senescent cells in the signalling centre that directs limb growth, where loss of p21 caused defects in limb patterning.[^storer2013]

## Senescent cells and ageing

### Accumulation

Senescence markers become more common with age in several human tissues, including skin, kidney and pancreas, but not in all; in lung they did not change, and skeletal muscle had almost none.[^idda2020] A meta-analysis of human studies found a modest overall correlation between senescence markers and age, *r* = 0.28, that varied "considerably depending upon tissue type, tissue section, and marker used".[^tuttle2020]

How long senescent cells persist depends on how fast they are removed. Omer Karin, Uri Alon and colleagues induced senescence in the lungs of mice and found that senescent cells "turn over rapidly in young mice, with a half-life of days, but slow their own removal rate to a half-life of weeks in old mice": about 5 days at 3 months of age and about 25 days at 22 months.[^karin2019] Mice with impaired cytotoxic immune cells, lacking perforin, accumulate more senescent cells and have shorter lives.[^ovadya2018]

### A model of senescent-cell dynamics

Karin and colleagues proposed a model in which senescent cells are produced at a rate that rises in proportion to age and removed at a rate that saturates when they are numerous, as when the immune cells that remove them are fully occupied. Random fluctuations are added, and death occurs when the burden of senescent cells first exceeds a threshold. With parameters fitted to mice, the model reproduces the exponential rise of mortality with age, the [[Mortality rate models#gompertz-law|Gompertz law]].[^karin2019]

Figure 1 simulates the model for 20,000 mice. With the reported maximum removal rate of 0.15 a day, the median mouse lives 28.4 months, and the monthly mortality rate rises about tenfold between 15 and 21 months. Drag the removal rate down to 0.12 a day and the median lifespan falls to 24.5 months; raise it to 0.18 and it rises to 32.4. Because removal saturates, a small loss of removal capacity late in life lets the burden rise steeply.

> [!note]- The saturating-removal model
> The senescent-cell burden $X$ changes as
>
> $$
> \frac{\mathrm{d}X}{\mathrm{d}t} = \eta\,t - \frac{\beta X}{\kappa + X} + \sqrt{2\varepsilon}\,\xi(t),
> $$
>
> where $\eta t$ is the production rate, rising with age $t$; $\beta$ is the maximum removal rate; $\kappa$ is the burden at which removal runs at half its maximum; and $\xi(t)$ is white noise of strength $\varepsilon$. Death occurs when $X$ first reaches $X_c$.[^karin2019] Figure 1 uses $\eta = 0.084$ per day per year, $\kappa = 0.5$, $\varepsilon = 0.16$ per day and $X_c = 17$, with $X$ in arbitrary units, and integrates the equation in half-day steps.

### Clearing senescent cells in mice

Darren Baker, Jan van Deursen and colleagues built mice in which cells expressing p16 can be killed with a drug (see [[baker-ink-attac-experiment]]). In mice with a progeroid mutation, life-long removal of these cells delayed age-related disease in fat, muscle and eye, and removal late in life "attenuated progression of already established age-related disorders".[^baker2011] In normal mice, removal twice a week from 12 months of age extended median lifespan by 27% in one genetic background and 24% in another, delayed tumour formation and attenuated the deterioration of kidney, heart and fat.[^baker2016] Removing cells that express high levels of p21, once a month from 20 months of age, extended both median and maximum lifespan.[^wang2024]

Transplanting small numbers of senescent cells into young mice caused persistent physical dysfunction and spread senescence to the host's tissues. Ming Xu and colleagues treated old mice with the senolytic combination of dasatinib and quercetin, which "increased post-treatment survival by 36% while reducing mortality hazard to 65%".[^xu2018]

Not all senescent cells are harmful to remove. Most of the cells with high p16 that accumulate in old mice are endothelial cells lining the liver's blood vessels. Removing them disrupted the barriers between blood and tissue and caused fibrosis, and they were not replaced.[^grosse2020] Clearing senescent cells from skin wounds delays healing.[^demaria2014]

## Senolytic drugs

Yi Zhu, James Kirkland and colleagues found that senescent cells depend on survival pathways that, when blocked, kill them but not dividing or quiescent cells. Dasatinib, a cancer drug, and quercetin, a plant flavonoid, each killed some types of senescent cells, and together they reduced the senescent-cell burden in old and progeroid mice.[^zhu2015] Navitoclax (ABT-263), an inhibitor of the BCL-2 family of survival proteins, cleared senescent blood and muscle stem cells in old and irradiated mice.[^chang2016] Fisetin, another flavonoid, extended median and maximum lifespan when fed to old mice,[^yousefzadeh2018] but the National Institute on Aging's Interventions Testing Program did not reproduce a lifespan effect in genetically diverse mice.[^harrison2024]

Human studies are early.

- **Pulmonary fibrosis.** In an open-label pilot study of 14 people with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, nine doses of dasatinib and quercetin over three weeks improved walking distance, gait speed and chair-standing time; lung function did not change. There was no placebo group.[^justice2019]
- **Diabetic kidney disease.** In 9 people given three days of dasatinib and quercetin, senescent cells in fat and skin and several SASP factors in blood decreased within 11 days.[^hickson2019]
- **Osteoarthritis.** UBX0101, injected into the knee, reduced post-traumatic osteoarthritis in mice.[^jeon2017] In a placebo-controlled trial of 183 patients with painful knee osteoarthritis, a single injection showed "no statistically significant difference" from placebo in pain at 12 weeks, and development was stopped.[^unity2020]

Saranya Chaib, Tamara Tchkonia and Kirkland summarised the state of the field in 2022: results in animals "have facilitated progression to early-phase clinical trials evaluating the safety and efficacy of senolytics".[^chaib2022]

## Senescence in theories of ageing

Campisi proposed in 2005 that senescence is antagonistically pleiotropic: it promotes survival early in life by suppressing cancer, and limits it later as senescent cells accumulate.[^campisi2005] Silvia Giaimo and d'Adda di Fagagna questioned whether the evidence shows that the benefits fall mainly in youth and the costs mainly in old age.[^giaimo2012] Senescence also continues to suppress cancer in old organisms, which bears on whether removing senescent cells late in life is safe. (See [[antagonistic-pleiotropy-theory#cellular-senescence|Antagonistic pleiotropy]] and [[tumor-suppressor-theory-of-aging]].)

## History

### Replicative senescence

Hayflick and Moorhead established in 1961 that normal human cells have a finite lifetime in culture, and Hayflick related it to the number of divisions in 1965.[^hayflick1961][^hayflick1965] In 1995 Dimri and colleagues found a marker that identified senescent cells in tissue,[^dimri1995] and between 1990 and 2003 shortening telomeres and the DNA-damage response at chromosome ends were identified as the mechanism of the replicative limit.[^harley1990][^bodnar1998][^dadda2003]

### Senescence beyond the Hayflick limit

Oncogene-induced senescence was described in 1997,[^serrano1997] and in 2005 four studies found senescence in premalignant tumours.[^collado2005][^michaloglou2005][^chen2005][^braig2005] The SASP was named in 2008,[^coppe2008] and senescence in normal embryonic development was described in 2013.[^munozespin2013][^storer2013]

### Senescent cells as a target

In 2011 and 2016 Baker and colleagues showed that removing p16-expressing cells delays age-related disease and extends lifespan in mice,[^baker2011][^baker2016] and the first senolytic drugs were reported in 2015.[^zhu2015] The first human pilot studies were published in 2019,[^justice2019][^hickson2019] and the first placebo-controlled trial failed in 2020.[^unity2020]
