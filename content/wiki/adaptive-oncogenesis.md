# Adaptive oncogenesis

**Adaptive oncogenesis** is an evolutionary theory of cancer initiation holding that age- and insult-associated increases in malignancy stem primarily from shifts in somatic *selection* within degraded tissues, rather than from the steady accumulation of oncogenic mutations alone.[^4] Developed and tested by [James DeGregori](james-degregori.md), the model centers on how tissue integrity governs whether mutant clones expand or remain suppressed.

## Fitness landscapes of healthy and degraded tissues

Whether an oncogenic mutation helps or harms a somatic clone depends on the fitness landscape of its niche:

- **In young, undamaged tissue**, wild-type stem and progenitor cells are well adapted to local constraints. Mutations that perturb signaling, metabolism, or differentiation typically reduce cellular competitiveness, leaving mutant clones neutral or disadvantaged relative to healthy neighbors.[^1][^2]
- **In aged or perturbed tissue**, chronic inflammation, stromal remodeling, irradiation, smoking, or stem-cell attrition degrade the niche. Because wild-type progenitors lose proliferative vigor under those conditions, oncogenic mutations that bypass impaired cytokine or checkpoint pathways—such as restoring downstream survival signaling when host cues fail—gain a positive selection coefficient over declining normal competitors.[^2][^3]

This emphasis complements mutation-supply accounts. Whereas the [[Tomasetti–Vogelstein model]] and classical [clonal evolution](clonal-evolution.md) focus on cell-division counts and mutational diversification, adaptive oncogenesis accounts for why cancer incidence rises in steep parallel with physiological decline even though a large fraction of somatic mutations in self-renewing tissues accumulates earlier in life. Both adaptive oncogenesis and the [[tumor-suppressor-theory-of-aging|tumor suppressor theory of aging]] treat vigorous wild-type tissue organization as an active anti-supercompetitor barrier whose breakdown permits clonal takeover.

## Experimental evidence

Competitive bone-marrow transplantation and chimeric progenitor assays in mice directly separate mutational supply from niche selection:

- **Age-dependent oncogene selection.** Aged mouse B-lymphoid progenitors show reduced receptor-associated kinase signaling and diminished competitiveness relative to young cells. Introducing the *Bcr-Abl* oncogene conferred little advantage in healthy young progenitors, whereas in aged or IL-7-deprived progenitors it restored Akt and STAT5 signaling and drove leukemic expansion.[^2]
- **Inflammation as a selective switch.** Systemic inflammation characteristic of old age suppresses wild-type B-progenitor competitiveness and favors oncogenic clone outgrowth; dampening inflammation restores normal progenitor fitness and limits leukemogenesis without lowering mutation rates.[^3]
- **Insult-driven niche remodeling.** Prior ionizing irradiation impairs the competitive fitness of unmutated hematopoietic stem cells, selecting for *p53*-deficient progenitors primarily because radiation weakens wild-type competitors rather than solely through *de novo* mutagenesis.[^1]

## Therapeutic and preventive implications

If microenvironmental deterioration unlocks oncogenic selection, cancer prevention extends beyond avoiding mutagens: preserving tissue homeostasis, reducing chronic sterile inflammation, and supporting normal stem-cell competitiveness can keep pre-existing mutant clones suppressed below clinical thresholds.[^3][^4]
