# Adaptive oncogenesis

Adaptive oncogenesis is an evolutionary theory of cancer initiation holding that age- and insult-associated increases in malignancy stem primarily from shifts in somatic *selection* within degraded tissues, rather than from the steady accumulation of mutations alone. [James DeGregori](james-degregori.md) formulated and experimentally tested the framework.

## The argument

Whether an oncogenic mutation helps or harms a clone depends on the fitness landscape of its host tissue:

- **In young, undamaged tissue**, wild-type stem and progenitor cells are already well adapted to their niche. An oncogenic mutation that alters proliferation, differentiation, or signaling often compromises metabolic efficiency or cell–niche integration, leaving mutated clones neutral or *maladaptive* so they are outcompeted or held to small clones by healthy neighbors.
- **In aged or perturbed tissue**, tissue homeostasis deteriorates through chronic inflammation, stromal remodeling, irradiation, smoking, or stem-cell pool attrition. Because wild-type progenitors lose competitive vigor in the degraded niche, oncogenic mutations that bypass the damaged regulatory pathway (for example, restoring downstream survival or growth signaling despite cytokine deprivation) suddenly become *adaptive* relative to declining normal competitors, enabling clonal expansion and eventual transformation.

The theory complements mutation-centric accounts cleanly: the [[Tomasetti–Vogelstein model]] and classical [clonal evolution](clonal-evolution.md) focus on mutation supply and clonal divergence, whereas adaptive oncogenesis explains why cancer incidence rises steeply with age in lockstep with physiological frailty despite half or more of somatic point mutations in renewing tissues already occurring earlier in life, and why tissues with vastly different division counts do not scale proportionally in malignancy risk. Notice the close kinship with the [[tumor-suppressor-theory-of-aging|tumor suppressor theory of aging]]: in both frameworks, maintaining robust wild-type somatic vigor and anti-supercompetitor tissue architecture is central, whereas declining tissue competitiveness invites clonal takeovers.

## Experimental evidence

DeGregori's laboratory tested these predictions directly using competitive bone-marrow transplantation and chimeric hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell assays in mice:

- **Age-dependent Bcr-Abl selection (*PNAS*, 2010).** Henry, Marusyk, & DeGregori demonstrated that aged B-lymphoid progenitors suffer reduced receptor-associated kinase signaling and impaired competitive fitness. Introducing the *Bcr-Abl* oncogene into young progenitors barely expanded clones in healthy young bone marrow, whereas *Bcr-Abl* restored defective Akt/STAT5 signaling in old progenitors — or under IL-7-limited stress — giving mutant cells a large selective advantage over aged neighbors and driving leukemogenesis.
- **Inflammation as a selective switch (*JCI*, 2015).** Henry, Casás-Selves, Kim, et al. further showed that aging-associated systemic inflammation is sufficient to degrade normal B-progenitor competitiveness and actively select for adaptive oncogenic clones, whereas suppressing chronic inflammation preserved wild-type progenitor fitness and blunted mutant clonal expansion without lowering mutation rates.
- **Insult-driven fitness remodeling (*PLoS Biology*, 2010).** Marusyk & DeGregori showed that prior ionizing irradiation impairs the competitive fitness of wild-type hematopoietic stem cells, selecting for *p53*-deficient progenitors not merely because radiation caused mutations, but because radiation damaged the unmutated competitor pool.

DeGregori synthesized this line of experiments and evolutionary logic in *Adaptive Oncogenesis: A New Understanding of How Cancer Evolves Inside Us* (Harvard University Press, 2018).

## Practical implications

If degraded tissue architecture and weak wild-type competition unlock oncogenic selection, cancer prevention expands beyond avoiding mutagens: preserving tissue homeostasis, dampening chronic sterile inflammation, and restoring normal stem-cell competitiveness hold mutant clones below clinical thresholds even when driver mutations already exist in aged organs.
